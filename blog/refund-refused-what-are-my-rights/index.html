My refund was refused. What are my rights?

When a company says no to a refund, it's easy to assume that's the end of it. Often it isn't. What matters is which problem you have, who you bought from and how you paid.

8 November 20266 min read
The short answer

A refused refund is the company's answer, not necessarily the final one. If something you paid a business for was faulty, not as described, or never arrived, you usually have legal rights that sit alongside its refund policy, and if you paid by card there may be a second route through your bank.

What decides the outcome is usually which problem you have, who you actually bought from, how you paid and what evidence you kept.

A "no" from customer service isn't the end

It's one of the questions we see most often on UK consumer forums and social media: an order arrives with items missing, the wrong thing turns up, something is broken out of the box, or nothing arrives at all. The person asks for their money back and gets a refusal, often an automated one, sometimes citing a policy, a time window or their "claims history".

A refusal like that is a company applying its own process. It tells you what the company is prepared to do without being pushed. It doesn't tell you what you're entitled to. Those are two different questions, and it's worth separating them before you decide whether to let it go.

First, work out which problem you have

Different problems are handled in different ways, so it helps to name yours plainly.

  • It never arrived. Citizens Advice says that if you bought something from a business to be delivered, it's the seller's responsibility to make sure it reaches you. If the seller used a courier, chasing the courier is their job, not yours.
  • Part of it is missing, or it's the wrong item. You paid for something you didn't receive, or received something that doesn't match what was described. Goods should match the seller's description.
  • It's broken, damaged or doesn't work. Goods bought from a business should be of satisfactory quality and fit for purpose. Citizens Advice describes "broken or damaged" as not of satisfactory quality, and "unusable" as not fit for purpose.
  • Nothing is wrong, you've just changed your mind. That's a separate set of rules for things bought online or by phone, and some items, such as perishable and personalised goods, are excluded.
Where your rights don't reach Citizens Advice notes that your rights don't cover damage caused by wear and tear, an accident or misuse, or a fault you knew about before you bought the item. And if you told a courier to leave a parcel somewhere and it went missing from there, the seller may not be responsible.

Who you actually bought from

This trips a lot of people up. On apps and marketplaces, the brand on the screen isn't always the business you have a contract with. It might be the platform itself, or a separate seller using it. The order confirmation and the platform's terms usually say which, and that tells you who to direct your complaint to.

The same goes for manufacturers. A manufacturer's warranty is an extra. Citizens Advice suggests considering your legal rights rather than relying only on a warranty, especially early on, and the business that sold to you is the usual starting point.

Timing matters too. Citizens Advice says it's usually easier to get a repair or replacement within the first six months; after that you may have to show you didn't cause the problem. You have legal rights over a fault for up to six years (five in Scotland), but the longer you leave it, the harder it tends to be.

How you paid can open a second route

If the seller won't move, the way you paid may give you another way to get your money back.

How you paidWhat may be available
Credit card, item costing more than £100 and not more than £30,000A Section 75 claim against your card provider for the seller's breach of contract or misrepresentation
Debit card, or credit card where Section 75 doesn't applyA chargeback request through your bank (usually within around 120 days)
PayPalA dispute through PayPal, which Citizens Advice says you can open within 180 days of paying

Two cautions. Section 75 isn't usually available when you paid through a third party, such as some marketplaces or PayPal. And a chargeback isn't a legal right in the same way: the Financial Ombudsman says a bank doesn't have to raise one, though it can be a complaint if the bank didn't when it would have been good practice.

If your card provider turns down a Section 75 or chargeback claim, you can complain to them formally. If you get no final response within eight weeks, or you're unhappy with it, the Financial Ombudsman Service can look at it.

What usually goes wrong

Most refused refunds that could have been recovered fall down for the same few reasons:

  • Evidence wasn't kept. Photos of what arrived, the packaging, the delivery notification, the order confirmation. Once an item is binned, it's much harder to show what was wrong with it.
  • The complaint went to the wrong place. Complaining to a courier, a manufacturer or the wrong party on a marketplace can burn weeks.
  • Everything happened in a chat window. Live chats and app messages are easy to lose. A written complaint with a clear account and a response deadline is harder to ignore.
  • The policy was treated as the law. A refund window or "claims history" rule is the company's own process. It may still matter, but it isn't automatically the end of the question.
  • People waited too long. Card routes in particular have time limits, and memories and evidence fade.
Before you do anything else Gather what you have in one place: the order, the payment, photos, and every reply you've had, with dates. Most of the hard work in a complaint is getting the facts straight.

Getting it right for your situation

The general picture is the easy part. The hard part is applying it to one real case: which of your rights actually fits what happened, whether your evidence is strong enough, who exactly to send it to, what to say, and where to go next if they still say no. A complaint that names the right problem, the right party and the right route tends to get taken more seriously than an angry message that covers everything.

Related questions

Can a company refuse a refund because of its own policy?
A company's refund policy is its own process. It doesn't replace your legal rights when goods are faulty, not as described or not delivered, and it doesn't stop you using card protections such as Section 75 or a chargeback where they apply.
Is the seller or the delivery company responsible if my order didn't arrive?
Citizens Advice says that if you bought from a business, it's the seller's responsibility to make sure the item is delivered to you, and chasing a courier is their job. The exception is usually where you told the courier to leave it somewhere and it went missing from there.
Can I use Section 75 if I paid by debit card?
No. Section 75 applies to credit card purchases costing more than £100 and not more than £30,000. With a debit card you can ask your bank for a chargeback instead, though the bank doesn't have to raise one.
What if my bank refuses my chargeback or Section 75 claim?
Make a formal complaint to the bank or card provider first. If they don't send a final response within eight weeks, or you're unhappy with it, you can take the complaint to the Financial Ombudsman Service.
Can I get a refund on food or personalised items if I change my mind?
Usually not. The online cancellation rules exclude some items, such as perishable and personalised goods. That is different from an order that was missing, wrong or not delivered, where your other rights still apply.
