I want to change career but don't know what to do. Where do I start?

Feeling stuck is common. Not knowing what to switch to is even more common. Here's how to think a career change through, starting from what you already have.

10 November 20266 min read
The short answer

Not knowing what you want next is normal, and it isn't a sign you should stay put. A good starting point is what you already have: the skills that travel, the parts of work you enjoy, and the things you want to leave behind.

From there, most people get clearer by testing a direction in small ways and getting independent advice, rather than waiting to feel certain.

Why "I don't know what I want" is so common

It's one of the questions we see most often on UK jobs and careers forums. Someone has been in the same line of work for years. It's stable, it may even pay well, and yet they feel stuck. They know they want something different. What they can't say is what.

That gap is normal. Most of us chose our first jobs with very little information, often by accident, and then kept building on them. By the time we want to change, our skills, our income and how other people see us are all tied to that first choice. So the real question usually isn't "what do I want to do?" It's "what could I move to, from here, that fits who I am now?"

Framed that way, it becomes something you can work on, rather than something you need to suddenly know.

Start with what you already have

People thinking about a change tend to focus on what they lack: the qualification, the experience, the contacts. It's usually more useful to start with what you already bring.

  • Transferable skills. Managing people, handling difficult customers, organising busy schedules, working with numbers, explaining complicated things simply. These travel between jobs far more than job titles suggest.
  • What you actually enjoy. Not the job as a whole, but the parts of it. The days that go quickly. The tasks you'd happily do more of.
  • What you want to leave behind. Sitting at a desk all day, an unpredictable income, shift patterns, no clear progression. Knowing what you're moving away from narrows the field quickly.
  • Your real constraints. How much you need to earn, where you can work, and how much time and money you could put into retraining.
A career change rarely starts from zero. It starts from everything you've done so far, described in a new way.

Three kinds of move

It helps to know which kind of change you're considering, because each comes with different trade-offs.

Type of moveWhat it looks likeWhat usually matters most
Same skills, new settingDoing similar work in a different industry or sectorShowing how your experience fits a new context
Next doorA related role that builds on what you already knowFilling one or two specific gaps
A real leapA completely different field, often with new trainingTime, money and evidence that you're committed

None of these is better than the others. But the first two are usually quicker and less risky, and they're often overlooked by people who assume a change has to be dramatic to count.

What usually holds people back

The same few obstacles come up again and again:

  • Waiting for certainty. It's rare to feel completely sure before a change. Most people get clearer by trying things, not by thinking harder.
  • Describing yourself in the old language. If your CV and your conversations are full of the terms of your current field, employers in a new one can struggle to see the connection.
  • Jumping straight to an expensive course. Retraining can be the right move, but committing money and months before you've tested the new direction is a big bet.
  • Ignoring the money question. A move that means a pay cut, even a temporary one, needs planning. Pretending it won't matter is a common reason changes get abandoned halfway.
  • Going it alone. Talking to people who already do the job you're curious about can save months of guessing.
The "apply for everything" trap When you're unsure what you want, it's tempting to apply widely and see what sticks. The trouble is that applications without a clear direction tend to look unfocused to employers, and a run of rejections can make you feel you have nothing to offer, when the real issue is the lack of a target.

Ways to test a direction

Before committing, most people benefit from finding small, low-risk ways to try an idea out. In general terms, that might mean:

  1. Read job adverts in the field. Look at what employers actually ask for, not what you assume they want.
  2. Talk to people who do the work. A short conversation about what the job is really like is worth a lot of research.
  3. Try a small piece of it. A short course, some volunteering, a side project or a day of shadowing can show you whether you like the work before you change your life for it.
  4. Get independent advice. A careers adviser can help you think it through without any interest in which way you go.
Careers help in the UK In England, the National Careers Service offers careers information and advice. If you're 18 or older, you can call 0800 072 0288 to speak to an adviser; the lines are open 8am to 6pm, Monday to Friday, and calls are free from landlines and most mobiles. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own services: My World of Work, Careers Wales and NI Direct's careers service.

It's also worth being kind to yourself about timing. Feeling stuck doesn't mean you've left it too late, and wanting a change doesn't mean your current job was a mistake. Both are just information about where you are now.

Getting it right for your situation

The general picture is the easy part. The hard part is applying it to one real person: which of your strengths actually carry over, which move is realistic from where you are, what's most likely to get in the way, and how to describe yourself so that a new field sees the fit. Two people with the same job title can have very different routes out of it.

Related questions

Am I too old to change career?
There's no fixed age. People change direction in their thirties, forties and later. What tends to matter more than age is how far the new role is from your current skills, how much retraining it needs, and whether you can afford the time and any drop in pay while you make the move.
Do I need to retrain to change career?
Not always. Some moves rely mostly on skills you already have, described in the language of the new field. Others, especially regulated professions, need a specific qualification. It's worth checking what the roles you're looking at actually require before committing to a course.
Should I quit my job before changing career?
Many people find it easier to explore while still employed: talking to people in the new field, taking short courses or trying small pieces of work. Leaving first can give you time, but it also adds money pressure that can push you into the first thing that comes along.
Where can I get careers advice in the UK?
In England, the National Careers Service offers careers information and advice, and adults aged 18 or older can call 0800 072 0288 to speak to an adviser. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own services: My World of Work, Careers Wales and NI Direct's careers service.
Is being made redundant a good time to change career?
It can be a natural moment to rethink, because the decision to leave has already been made for you. The same cautions apply: be clear about your finances, look at what your experience already fits, and test a new direction before committing heavily to it.
