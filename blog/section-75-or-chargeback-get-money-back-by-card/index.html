Section 75 or chargeback: can my card company get my money back?

When a seller won't refund you, your bank or card provider may be a second place to turn. There are two routes, and they work very differently.

20 November 20266 min read
The short answer

If you paid by credit card and the item or service cost more than £100 and not more than £30,000, Section 75 can make your card provider liable alongside the seller, even if only a deposit went on the card. If you paid by debit card, or Section 75 doesn't fit, you can ask your bank for a chargeback, but that isn't a legal right and it has a tight time limit. Which route works depends on how you paid, who you paid and what went wrong.

Why the way you paid matters

It's a question we see often on UK consumer forums: a builder leaves a job half finished and stops answering, a company takes a deposit and never turns up to install, or a delivery app refuses a refund and the person says they will "just do a chargeback". Card protection comes up again and again. It's also widely misunderstood.

The reason the payment method matters is simple. If you paid the seller directly, your only claim is against them. If you paid with a card, there may be a second party who can be asked to put things right: the bank or card provider that handled the money. There are two quite different ways of doing that, and they work in different ways.

Section 75: a legal right on credit card purchases

Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 makes a credit card provider liable, alongside the seller, if the seller breaks the contract or misrepresents what you were buying. In law the two are "jointly and severally liable". In plain terms, Which? describes it as the card company sharing equal responsibility with the trader, so you have the same rights against the card company as you have against the seller.

The main conditions are these:

  • A credit card, not a debit card. Which? notes Section 75 applies only to credit cards, not to debit cards or charge cards.
  • The item must cost more than £100 and not more than £30,000. It's the price of the single item or service that counts, not the whole order and not the amount you put on the card.
  • Part payment is enough. If you paid a deposit on your credit card and the rest another way, you can usually claim for the full cost.
  • It covers services as well as goods. Poor workmanship, a service that wasn't provided, or something that wasn't as described can all fall within it.
  • It can help when the seller has gone bust. UK Finance says you may be able to claim even if the company has ceased trading.

UK Finance also says you should make a Section 75 claim within six years of buying, or of when you were due to receive what you paid for. That is a long window, but evidence gets harder to gather the longer you leave it.

The third-party trap Section 75 relies on a direct link between you, the card provider and the seller. Citizens Advice says you can't usually use it if you paid through a third party rather than directly to the trader, for example some PayPal payments or some marketplaces. That's often where claims fall down.

Chargeback: a card scheme process, not a law

Chargeback is the route most people mean when they talk about "getting the bank to reverse it". It isn't written into law. It comes from the rules of the card schemes, such as Visa, Mastercard and American Express, and it works on debit cards as well as credit cards.

That makes it useful for smaller amounts, for debit card payments, and for cases where Section 75 doesn't fit. But it has limits that people often don't expect:

  • It is time limited. UK Finance says the process usually has to start within 120 days of the transaction or of when you were due to receive the goods or services.
  • There's no guarantee. Your bank may decide not to pursue it, and UK Finance says there are no guarantees the money can be recovered.
  • The seller can fight back. Citizens Advice warns that a trader can challenge a chargeback, even after you've had the money back.

What usually goes wrong

Most card claims that fail do so for a handful of reasons:

  • The wrong route. Asking for a chargeback on a large credit card purchase where Section 75 would have given a legal right, or trying Section 75 on a debit card or a third-party payment.
  • Leaving it too late. People spend months chasing the seller and miss the chargeback window.
  • No clear account of what went wrong. The card provider needs to see what was agreed and how the seller fell short. "The service was awful" isn't the same as showing a breach of contract.
  • Thin evidence. UK Finance says you will need to prove your claim. Quotes, contracts, receipts, photos, messages and dates all matter.
  • Trying to be paid twice. You can't recover the same money from both the seller and the card provider, and Citizens Advice notes you can only get a Section 75 refund from one card provider.
Should you go to the seller first? Citizens Advice says it's usually best to contact the trader first, and UK Finance says it can often be quicker. But Which? points out you don't have to reach a stalemate with the seller before going to your credit card provider under Section 75.

If the card provider says no

A refusal from your bank or card provider isn't necessarily the end either. You can make a formal complaint to it. Which? says the provider then has eight weeks to deal with your complaint, and if it doesn't, or you're unhappy with its final answer, you can take the complaint to the Financial Ombudsman Service.

Getting it right for your situation

The rules above are the general picture. Applying them to one real case is harder: whether your payment meets the conditions, whether Section 75 or chargeback fits better, whether what happened counts as a breach of contract, what evidence you need, and how to put it so the card provider can see the claim clearly. Getting the route or the account wrong is the most common reason a claim stalls.

Related questions

Can I use Section 75 if I only paid a deposit on my credit card?
Yes, as long as the item or service itself cost more than £100 and not more than £30,000. Which? and Citizens Advice both say the card company can be liable for the full cost even if only part of it, such as a deposit, went on the credit card.
Does Section 75 work if the company has gone bust?
It can. UK Finance says you may be able to claim even if the company has gone out of business and stopped trading, because your claim is against the card provider as well as the seller.
Is there a time limit for a chargeback?
Chargeback runs on card scheme rules rather than law. UK Finance says the process usually has to start within 120 days of the transaction or of when you were due to receive the goods or services, so it pays to act quickly.
Can I use Section 75 if I paid through PayPal?
Usually not. Citizens Advice says you can't usually use Section 75 if you paid through a third party rather than directly to the trader. A chargeback or the third party's own dispute process may still be available.
What if my card provider turns my claim down?
Complain to the card provider formally. Which? says it then has eight weeks to deal with your complaint, and if you are unhappy with its answer, or it runs out of time, you can take it to the Financial Ombudsman Service.
