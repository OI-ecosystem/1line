My energy bill is wrong and my supplier won't fix it. What can I do?

A bill that doesn't match your meter, a catch-up bill for months you thought were paid, or credit the supplier won't return. These disputes are common, and there is a clear route for them, even when customer service goes quiet.

11 November 20266 min read
The short answer

A wrong bill is something your supplier has to put right, but it usually takes a clear complaint, backed by meter readings, to get there. If the supplier hasn't resolved it within 8 weeks, or sends you a deadlock letter, you can take it to the Energy Ombudsman, which is free.

Two rules help many people: suppliers usually can't bill you for energy used more than 12 months ago if they didn't bill you correctly at the time, and you can ask for credit back whenever you like.

Why energy bills go wrong

It's one of the questions we see most often on UK money forums and social media. A bill arrives that's far higher than anything the household has used before. A final bill turns up long after a move or a switch. A smart meter turns out to be linked to the wrong property. Or a large credit sits on the account and the supplier won't hand it back.

Citizens Advice lists the usual causes: an estimated bill, a price change, the wrong meter on your account, a faulty meter, or a plain mistake by the supplier. Meter faults are rare. Estimates and admin errors are not.

Working out which one you're dealing with matters, because each is fixed in a different way:

  • An estimated bill. Citizens Advice says that if an estimate is too high, you should take a meter reading and send it in, and the supplier should send an updated, accurate bill.
  • The wrong meter. Every meter has a reference number: the MPRN for gas and the MPAN for electricity. Citizens Advice suggests checking the number on your bill against the meter registered to your address. If they don't match, you may be paying for someone else's energy.
  • A supplier mistake. Tell the supplier you think you've been overcharged and send photos of your meter readings as proof.
Start with a dated photo A clear, dated photo of each meter is the single most useful piece of evidence in a billing dispute. Take one now, even if you've already sent readings before.

The 12-month back-billing rule

Catch-up bills are where many disputes start. Ofgem's rule, in place since 2018, bans domestic suppliers from issuing back-bills for energy used more than 12 months ago where they hadn't billed you correctly at the time. Citizens Advice puts it simply: your supplier usually can't charge you for energy you used more than a year ago, and if you pay by direct debit, it shouldn't raise your payments to cover energy used over 12 months ago.

There are limits to know about:

  • It isn't a free pass on recent usage. Energy used within the last 12 months can still be billed, even if it comes as one larger bill after a long gap. Citizens Advice says you'll need to pay that, though you can get help if you can't pay it in full.
  • Obstruction is the main exception. Ofgem says the rule doesn't protect customers who actively prevent accurate readings, for example by blocking access to the meter or tampering with it. Ofgem is clear that simply not sending readings doesn't put you at fault.
  • Old bills that were sent properly still count. Suppliers can still chase unpaid bills they issued correctly at the time.

Because the rule turns on dates, when the energy was used and when it was first correctly billed, many people aren't sure whether it applies to them. Lining up your bills and readings in order usually makes that much clearer.

Credit the supplier won't return

If your account says you're "in credit", the supplier owes you money. Citizens Advice says you can ask for credit back at any time: contact the supplier, say how much you'd like refunded, and give an up-to-date meter reading.

A supplier can refuse if it has a good reason, but Citizens Advice says it must explain its decision. A refusal with no reason, or one you think is wrong, is something you can complain about through the same route as a billing error.

How the complaints route works

Energy complaints follow a set path, and knowing where you are on it takes a lot of the frustration out of the wait.

  1. Complain to the supplier. Make it clear you're raising a formal complaint, not just a query. Keep every email, chat and letter, and note the date and what was said on each call.
  2. Allow 8 weeks. The Energy Ombudsman says suppliers have 8 weeks to resolve a dispute. After that, the supplier can't stop you taking it further.
  3. Or get a deadlock letter. If the supplier sends a deadlock letter, saying it can't do any more, you can go to the ombudsman before the 8 weeks are up.
  4. Go to the Energy Ombudsman. It's free. You must escalate within 12 months of the deadlock letter, or of the supplier telling you its decision.

The Energy Ombudsman can ask the supplier to take practical steps, such as applying a credit, correcting a tariff or closing an account. It can also require an apology and a financial award for the time and trouble caused. If you accept its decision, the supplier must carry it out within 28 days. If you don't accept it, you're free to pursue the complaint another way, such as through the courts.

Keep paying while you dispute The Energy Ombudsman doesn't recommend cancelling direct debits or refusing to pay bills while a dispute is being investigated. Challenging the amount and keeping your account in order can happen at the same time.

What usually goes wrong

  • The complaint never officially starts. Weeks of calls and chats can pass without anything being logged as a complaint, so the 8-week clock hasn't started.
  • No readings to back it up. Without dated readings or photos, it's your word against the supplier's records.
  • The dates aren't lined up. Back-billing, credit and escalation all depend on dates. A muddled history makes a strong case look weak.
  • Accepting the first goodwill offer without checking it. A small gesture may not reflect the error. It's worth knowing what you're giving up before you agree to close a complaint.
  • Missing the 12-month window. Leaving a deadlock letter in a drawer can close off the ombudsman route.

Getting it right for your situation

The general picture is the easy part. The hard part is applying it to one real account: which rule fits what happened, whether your readings and bills actually show it, what to ask for, and whether you're ready to escalate. A complaint that sets out the facts in order, with the evidence attached and a clear request, tends to get further than another call to customer service.

Related questions

Can my energy supplier bill me for energy I used more than a year ago?
Usually not. Ofgem's back-billing rule stops domestic suppliers billing for energy used more than 12 months ago if they didn't bill you correctly at the time. The main exception is where the customer actively prevented accurate readings, for example by obstructing access to the meter or tampering with it.
How long does my supplier have to sort out my complaint?
The Energy Ombudsman says suppliers have 8 weeks to resolve a dispute. After that, or sooner if the supplier sends you a deadlock letter, you can take the complaint to the Energy Ombudsman.
Is there a time limit for going to the Energy Ombudsman?
Yes. The Energy Ombudsman says you must escalate within 12 months of receiving a deadlock letter. Citizens Advice gives the same 12 months from when your supplier tells you its decision.
Should I stop paying while I dispute my bill?
The Energy Ombudsman doesn't recommend cancelling direct debits or refusing to pay bills while a dispute is being investigated. Disputing the amount and keeping your account in order can go side by side.
Can I get my energy credit refunded?
Citizens Advice says you can ask for credit back at any time, giving an up-to-date meter reading. A supplier can refuse if it has a good reason, but it must explain its decision, and you can complain if you disagree.
