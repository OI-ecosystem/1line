How do I appeal a private parking charge?

A letter demanding £100 for a short stop in a car park can feel like a done deal. It often isn't. What matters is who sent it, what the signs said and whether the rules were followed on both sides.

12 November 20266 min read
The short answer

A private parking charge is a claim under a contract with the landowner or parking company, not a council fine. If the company belongs to an accredited trade association, you appeal to the company first and, if it says no, to an independent appeals service, within set time limits.

Appeals tend to succeed or fail on the same few things: the signs, whether you actually broke the rules, whether the paperwork was sent on time, and what evidence you kept.

First, check what kind of ticket you have

It's one of the questions we see most often on UK motoring and money forums: a letter arrives weeks after a short stop in a supermarket, retail park or residential car park, asking for £100 or more. The first job is to work out who sent it, because the rules are different.

  • A Penalty Charge Notice (PCN) is usually issued by a council on public land. It has its own appeal process, ending with an independent tribunal.
  • A Parking Charge Notice is issued by a landowner or parking company on private land. Confusingly, it is often shortened to "PCN" too.

A private parking charge is not a fine. The government's Private Parking Code of Practice describes it as a fee or charge under a contract, or a sum in the nature of damages, however it is described, and says signs must not use the words "penalty" or "fine" unless the law requires it.

Don't pay if you mean to appeal Citizens Advice says paying is seen as admitting the ticket was right, so you won't be able to appeal it once you've paid.

Who you appeal to, and when

Start by checking whether the company is a member of an accredited trade association: the British Parking Association (BPA) or the International Parking Community (IPC). Citizens Advice says only members can get your name and address from the DVLA, and if the company isn't a member you shouldn't contact it unless it writes to you first.

If it is a member, the process has two stages.

  1. Appeal to the parking company. Citizens Advice says you must write to the company before you can make a formal appeal to an independent service.
  2. Appeal to the independent service if it says no. For BPA members that is POPLA, and you have 28 days from when your appeal to the company was rejected. For IPC members it is the Independent Appeals Service, where Citizens Advice says you can appeal free within 21 days.

Many companies offer a discount for paying quickly. Citizens Advice says private operators usually offer 40% to 60% off if you pay within 14 days, and you can often still get a reduced amount if you pay soon after an appeal to the company is rejected. That is a real choice to weigh: appealing may mean giving up the discount if you lose.

What usually gets a ticket cancelled

Citizens Advice lists the reasons a private ticket should be cancelled. The main ones are:

  • The signs. No signs, signs that were faded, hidden, misleading or confusing, or no sign saying parking was suspended. If the ticket came by post, there should have been signs saying cameras were in use.
  • You followed the rules. You paid, had a valid permit, or stayed within the time allowed.
  • There was no way to pay. For example, a broken machine with no other way to pay. This tends not to work if another machine or app was available.
  • You were charged too much. Citizens Advice says trade association rules mean you shouldn't be charged more than £100 unless the company can show a bigger loss.
  • You couldn't get back in time. For example, because of a disability or pregnancy, or because your car broke down.
  • You weren't driving, or the car wasn't yours. For instance, it had been sold, stolen or cloned.

Grace periods matter too. Citizens Advice says trade association members must give you an extra 10 minutes before issuing a ticket. Many disputes come down to a camera timing the stay from entry to exit, including time spent queuing, looking for a space or reading the signs. The Code says the time a vehicle entered the land must not, on its own, be treated as the start of the parking period.

Keeper, driver and the paperwork timetable

With a private ticket, the person who was driving is responsible. If the letter came to you as the registered keeper, Citizens Advice says you don't have to tell the company who was driving. If you do give the driver's full name and address, the company must cancel the ticket against you and reissue it to the driver.

In England and Wales, a company can only pursue the keeper rather than the driver if it follows the rules in Schedule 4 of the Protection of Freedoms Act 2012. One of them is timing. Where no ticket was left on the car, the notice to the keeper must be delivered within 14 days, starting the day after the parking ended. A posted notice is treated as delivered on the second working day after posting. Late or incomplete paperwork is one of the most common points raised in appeals, and it is easy to get wrong in either direction, so check dates carefully.

Before you write anything Write down the dates: when you parked, what the letter says, when it arrived and when it was posted. Take photos of the signs, the entrance and any machine, and keep your payment record. Most appeals are won or lost on facts gathered early.

What happens if you ignore it

Citizens Advice warns against ignoring a letter about a parking ticket, even if you've paid, because it can have serious consequences: if the company takes you to court and wins, you could face court costs, damage to your credit rating and, in the end, bailiffs. It says you can't be taken to court while you are appealing to the company, and that demands for payment while you're appealing can amount to harassment.

If an appeal fails, the parking company decides whether it is worth taking you to court. It pays to read every letter properly and keep to any dates in it.

Getting it right for your situation

The general rules are only half the picture. The hard part is applying them to one ticket: which grounds actually fit what happened, whether the notice arrived in time, whether the company is a member of a trade association, which appeal body and deadline apply, and what evidence would make the point. A short, factual appeal built on the right grounds usually gets further than a long, angry one that tries every argument at once.

Related questions

Is a private parking charge the same as a fine?
No. A council Penalty Charge Notice is a statutory penalty. A private Parking Charge Notice is a charge under a contract with the landowner or parking company, and the government's Code of Practice says signs must not call it a penalty or a fine unless the law requires it.
How long do I have to appeal a private parking ticket?
You appeal to the parking company first. If a BPA member rejects it, Citizens Advice says you have 28 days to appeal to POPLA. If an IPC member rejects it, you can appeal free to the Independent Appeals Service within 21 days.
Do I have to tell the parking company who was driving?
No. Citizens Advice says the keeper doesn't have to name the driver and can choose to pay instead. If you do give the driver's full name and address, the company must cancel the ticket against you and reissue it to the driver.
Should I just ignore a private parking ticket?
Citizens Advice advises against it. If the company takes you to court and wins, you could face court costs, damage to your credit rating and bailiffs. Appealing within the time limits, or paying, are the safer routes.
Is there a grace period in private car parks?
Citizens Advice says members of an accredited trade association must give you an extra 10 minutes before issuing a parking charge, and the Code says the time you entered the land shouldn't, on its own, count as the start of your parking.
