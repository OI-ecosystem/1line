My flight was delayed or cancelled. Can I claim compensation myself?

Many people hand a delayed-flight claim to a claims firm, or give up when the airline says no. Often neither is necessary. What matters is the flight, the length of the delay and the real reason for it.

9 November 20266 min read
The short answer

Often, yes. Under UK rules, if your flight was covered and you arrived more than three hours late, or it was cancelled at short notice, you may be owed a fixed sum per passenger, unless the airline can show extraordinary circumstances outside its control.

You claim from the airline yourself. If it refuses or goes quiet for eight weeks, there is a free escalation route, and the CAA says you don't need a claims management company to use it.

Why this question keeps coming up

It's one of the questions we see most often on UK travel and money forums: a flight is hours late, cancelled the day before, or rescheduled again and again. Then the claim goes in and comes back refused, part-paid, or not answered at all. People ask whether they're entitled to anything, whether the airline's reason holds up, and whether they should give a claims firm a share just to get it dealt with.

The rules are more settled than most people expect. The hard part isn't usually the law. It's working out which rule your flight falls under, and whether the airline's explanation actually fits.

When UK compensation applies

Your rights come from the UK version of the flight compensation rules, often called UK261. The CAA says they cover flights departing from a UK airport on any airline, flights arriving at a UK airport on a UK or EU airline, and flights arriving at an EU airport on a UK airline.

For a delay, the trigger is when you arrive, not when you leave: compensation may apply if you reach your destination airport more than three hours late. For a cancellation, the CAA says you may be able to claim if you were told less than 14 days before you were due to fly. The amounts are fixed and set by distance:

Flight distanceCompensation per person (delay of 3 hours or more on arrival)
Under 1,500km£220
1,500km to 3,500km£350
Over 3,500km£260 if 3 to 4 hours late, £520 if more than 4 hours late

Cancellations follow similar bands, with some amounts reduced if the replacement flight got you there close to your original times. On connecting flights booked as one journey, the CAA says distance is measured from the first airport to the last.

Where the rules don't reach The CAA says that if you booked connecting flights separately yourself (a self-transfer), there is no statutory right to care, compensation or transport for a missed connection. Compensation is also not automatic: you have to claim it.

Compensation, care and refunds are different things

A lot of confusion comes from treating these as one claim. They aren't.

  • Care is what the airline must give you while you wait: food and drink in line with the delay, a way to communicate, and a hotel and transport if you're stuck overnight. The CAA says this starts after 2, 3 or 4 hours depending on distance.
  • A refund or rerouting is your choice when a flight is cancelled: your money back for the unused part of the ticket, or another flight as soon as possible or at a later date that suits you. The CAA says that if a delay goes beyond five hours, you can choose not to travel and get a refund.
  • Compensation is the fixed sum above, paid for the disruption itself.

This matters because extraordinary circumstances can remove compensation but don't remove care or the refund and rerouting rights. An airline that says "it wasn't our fault" may be right about compensation and still owe you your hotel and meal costs.

"Extraordinary circumstances": when a refusal holds up

Most refused claims rest on this phrase. The CAA's examples include weather that makes flying unsafe, strikes by people outside the airline such as air traffic control, airport staff or border staff, security risks, political unrest, terrorism or sabotage, and hidden manufacturing defects.

Just as important is what usually doesn't count. The CAA says technical problems are generally not extraordinary, pointing to court decisions including Jet2 v Huzar. So "a technical issue" on its own is a weaker reason than many airlines' refusal emails suggest. On the other hand, when air traffic control systems fail, the CAA has said resulting delays are likely to be extraordinary circumstances, so compensation is unlikely, though care and refunds still apply.

A useful question to ask Was the reason given actually the cause of your flight's delay, or something that happened elsewhere that day? Airlines have to show the link, not just name an event.

If the airline says no, or says nothing

The CAA says you should take your complaint to the airline first, in your own name; complaints sent by third parties may be rejected. If the airline hasn't replied within eight weeks of your written complaint, or you're unhappy with its final answer, you can take it to alternative dispute resolution (ADR).

  • It's free. The CAA says ADR is free for consumers, and that you don't need a claims management company to use it. A claims firm may keep a significant share of any award.
  • It depends on the airline. UK airlines and airports are signed up to AviationADR or CEDR. If an airline isn't signed up to a scheme, the CAA's own complaints team can look at it, though it can't force a decision.
  • The decision usually binds the airline, not you. The CAA says the airline is required to follow the adjudicator's decision, but you don't have to accept it and can still go to court.
  • There are time limits. ADR covers issues from the last six years (five in Scotland), and you should apply within 12 months of the airline's final response.

What usually goes wrong

  • The wrong time is measured. Delay is counted at arrival, so a long wait at the gate followed by a fast flight can fall under three hours.
  • Only one passenger is claimed for. Compensation is per person, and part-payments for a group booking are common.
  • The refusal reason isn't questioned. A vague "operational" or "technical" reason may not meet the bar the CAA describes.
  • Care costs aren't claimed. Receipts for meals, taxis and hotels are often lost or never sent.
  • The eight weeks are never started. Chats and phone calls don't always count as a clear written complaint, so the clock to ADR never begins.

Getting it right for your situation

The general rules are the easy part. Applying them to one disrupted journey is harder: which rule covers your flight, whether the airline's reason really fits what happened, what you can show, which scheme your airline uses, and what to put in writing so a refusal can be taken further. A claim that gets those right tends to be taken more seriously than one that just asks for money back.

Related questions

Do I have to use a claims company to get flight delay compensation?
No. The CAA recommends complaining directly to the airline, and says you do not need a third-party claims management company to use alternative dispute resolution, which is free for consumers. A claims firm may deduct a significant proportion of any award.
Is compensation paid per passenger or per booking?
The CAA describes the fixed amounts as per person, so each eligible passenger on the booking has their own entitlement.
Can an airline refuse compensation because of a technical fault?
Usually not. The CAA says technical problems are generally not extraordinary circumstances, citing court cases including Jet2 v Huzar. The exceptions are things like a hidden manufacturing defect or damage from sabotage or terrorism.
Am I owed compensation if air traffic control caused the delay?
Usually not. The CAA lists strikes by air traffic control and other parties outside the airline as examples of extraordinary circumstances. The airline must still look after you during the delay and offer a refund or rerouting where the rules require it.
How far back can I go to complain about a delayed flight?
For the CAA-approved dispute schemes, the CAA says complaints can cover issues from the last six years (five if you live in Scotland), and should be submitted within 12 months of the airline's final response.
