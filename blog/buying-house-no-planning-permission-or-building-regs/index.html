Buying a house with no planning permission or building regs sign-off: how risky is it?

You love the house, then the searches show the loft conversion or extension was never signed off. It’s a common snag, and it can often be managed, but the rules on how long a council can act changed recently, and that matters for newer work.

24 November 20266 min read
The short answer

It can be a real risk, but it is often manageable. Planning permission and building regulations approval are two separate things, and a house can be missing either or both. As the owner, you become responsible for work done before you bought, and for newer work in England the council now has up to 10 years to act on both. The usual routes are getting the seller to regularise the work, getting evidence that it is lawful, or pricing in and insuring the risk with your solicitor’s help.

Two different approvals, often confused

It’s a question we see often on UK house-buying forums. A buyer finds a home with a loft room or a rear extension, the offer is accepted, and then the solicitor’s enquiries turn up a gap: no planning record, no building regulations certificate, or neither.

The first thing to untangle is which approval is missing, because they do different jobs. The Law Society’s notes on the TA6 property information form put it simply: planning permission is generally needed if works change the outside appearance of a building, and building regulations approval is different from planning permission.

  • Planning permission is about whether the work should exist at all: its size, look and effect on the area. Some work can be done without it, so a missing planning record is not always a breach.
  • Building regulations approval is about whether the work was built safely and properly: structure, fire escape, insulation, drainage, electrics. The Law Society says buyers will often need approvals and completion certificates under the Building Regulations for building work.

A loft conversion can need building regulations approval even where it needed no planning permission. So “it didn’t need planning” and “it was signed off” are two separate claims, and each needs its own evidence.

Why it becomes your problem once you buy

The reason this matters to a buyer, not just the seller, is that responsibility moves with the property. Lambeth Council says that as a property owner you are responsible for making sure work meets building regulations, even if that work took place before you bought. The Law Society says the same about planning: a buyer could face enforcement action for work carried out by a previous owner.

Enforcement can mean being required to alter or remove the work. Oxford City Council says a council can serve a notice under section 36 of the Building Act 1984 requiring the removal or alteration of work that breaks the regulations. Oxford also notes that unauthorised work can cause issues when selling a property, getting insurance, or making sure the work is safe.

Safety comes before paperwork A missing certificate is a legal and money question, but it can also hide a real problem: a loft without a safe escape route, or an extension built without proper foundations. A survey and the right questions matter as much as the documents.

How long the council has to act

This is where the rules changed, and it’s the part many older forum threads get wrong.

Planning, in England. The old “four-year rule” for building work has gone. The Planning Inspectorate says a single 10-year limit now applies to all breaches of planning control. That limit applies where the building work was substantially completed on or after 25 April 2024. Work finished before that date stays under the earlier rules, which is why the completion date of the work matters so much.

Building regulations. The window has also been widened. The Building Safety Act 2022 changed the time limit for a section 36 notice, which can require non-compliant work to be removed or altered, from 12 months to 10 years after the work was completed. It applies in England and Wales.

Listed buildings. The Law Society says there are no time limits for enforcement action for listed building breaches. If the house is listed, unapproved alterations are a more serious matter.

Find out when the work was finished A loft completed in 2022 and one completed in 2025 can sit under very different enforcement time limits. Dated invoices, photos, council tax records and the seller’s answers on the property information form can all help establish when work was done.

The main options buyers use

There is no single fix. In general, buyers and their solicitors weigh up some mix of these:

  • Ask the seller to regularise the building work. The Planning Portal says a regularisation application is a retrospective application for work done without building regulations consent, started on or after 11 November 1985. It applies even if the work was done before the current owner bought. Parts of the work may need to be exposed so it can be checked, and Oxford City Council says the certificate is evidence, but not conclusive evidence, that the work complies.
  • Seek evidence that the planning position is lawful. The Planning Portal says that if you want to be certain an existing use of a building is lawful for planning purposes, you can apply to the council for a lawful development certificate. It isn’t compulsory, and the application must give the council enough information to decide.
  • Ask about insurance. Solicitors sometimes suggest an indemnity policy for missing approvals. It deals with some of the financial risk, not the condition of the work. Ask your solicitor what it covers, what it excludes and what it needs from you to stay valid.
  • Reflect the risk in the price, or walk away. If the work can’t be shown to be safe or lawful, some buyers renegotiate and some decide the house isn’t right for them.

Your lender has a say too. A mortgage is secured on the property, so your solicitor will usually need the lender to be comfortable with how the issue is handled.

What usually goes wrong

  • Treating the two approvals as one. A planning answer doesn’t settle the building regulations question, and the reverse is also true.
  • Relying on old rules. Advice that “after four years you’re safe” doesn’t apply to building work completed in England on or after 25 April 2024.
  • Counting rooms that may not count. A loft room that doesn’t meet building regulations may not be valued as a proper bedroom, which affects what the house is worth to you and to the next buyer.
  • Leaving it late. Regularisation and certificates take time. Raising the issue as soon as it appears gives everyone room to deal with it before exchange.

Getting it right for your situation

The general picture is the easy part. The hard part is the money question underneath it: is the asking price treating that loft or extension as fully legitimate space? What is the house worth with it, and without it? And if you buy, how would the next buyer see it?

The legal side belongs with your solicitor, and the condition of the work with a surveyor. What a general guide can’t do is read one home against the sales around it. Related questions

Is a loft conversion without building regs a deal breaker?
Not always. Many buyers go ahead, but the risk depends on how recent the work is, whether it is safe, and whether your lender and solicitor are happy. The seller may be able to apply for a regularisation certificate, which looks at the work retrospectively.
Can the council take action against me for work the previous owner did?
Yes, it can. Lambeth Council says owners are responsible for building regulations compliance even if the work was done before they bought, and the Law Society says a buyer could face planning enforcement for work carried out by a previous owner.
How long does a council have to take planning enforcement action?
In England, the Planning Inspectorate says a single 10-year limit now applies to all planning breaches. It covers building work substantially completed on or after 25 April 2024. Older work stays under the earlier rules. Listed building breaches have no time limit.
What is a regularisation certificate?
It is a certificate from the council’s building control that covers work done without building regulations approval, retrospectively. The Planning Portal says it applies to work started on or after 11 November 1985, and parts of the work may need to be exposed so they can be checked.
What is a lawful development certificate?
It is a certificate from the council confirming that an existing use or development is lawful for planning purposes. The Planning Portal says it is not compulsory, but you must give the council enough information for it to decide.
