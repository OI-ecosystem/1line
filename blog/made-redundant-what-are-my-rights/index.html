I've been made redundant. What are my rights, and what should I do next?

Being told your job is going is a shock, and the days after it are when the most important decisions get made. Knowing the basic rules first makes it much easier to think about what comes next.

26 November 20266 min read
The short answer

If you are an employee, redundancy comes with a set of legal minimums: a fair process, a notice period, and usually statutory redundancy pay once you have worked for your employer for two years. Check what you are owed, keep an eye on the time limits, and then turn to the harder question: what kind of job you go for next and how you present yourself for it.

Why redundancy feels so hard to think through

It's a question we see often on UK work and careers forums: someone has just been told their role is at risk, or has already been given notice, sometimes after many years in the same job, and wants to know whether they're being treated fairly and what to do now.

Those are different problems, and mixing them up is where people come unstuck. The first has rules, figures and deadlines. The second has no rulebook at all. It helps to deal with the rights side quickly and clearly, so you can give the next step the attention it needs.

Where these rules apply The figures below come from GOV.UK and Acas, and apply in England, Scotland and Wales. Northern Ireland has its own redundancy rules on nidirect.

Your main rights during redundancy

A fair process

Your employer should consult you before making a final decision. Acas says that if fewer than 20 redundancies are proposed, your employer should inform and consult you individually, and that if they propose 20 or more at one establishment within 90 days, they must hold collective consultation. There's no fixed length in law for individual consultation, but Acas expects at least one private meeting.

How people are chosen matters too. Acas says employers often put similar roles into a "selection pool", and that the criteria should be fair, measurable and not based on personal opinion. It's against the Equality Act 2010 to select someone because of a protected characteristic such as age, disability, pregnancy and maternity, race or sex. Attendance can be used, but Acas says absences linked to disability, pregnancy or maternity must not count.

Notice

Acas sets out the statutory minimum notice an employer must give:

How long you've worked thereMinimum notice
1 month to 2 years1 week
2 to 12 years1 week for each full year
12 years or more12 weeks

Your contract may give you more, never less, so it's worth checking.

Time off to look for work

If you are an employee with two years' continuous service, Acas says your employer must allow you a reasonable amount of time off during your notice period to look for a job or arrange training. You need to ask for it during your notice. The law only requires pay for this time up to 40% of a week's pay, unless your contract says more.

Redundancy pay: what you're entitled to

GOV.UK says you will normally get statutory redundancy pay if you are an employee and have worked for your current employer for two years or more. It's based on your age during each full year of service:

  • half a week's pay for each full year you were under 22
  • one week's pay for each full year you were 22 or older, but under 41
  • one and a half weeks' pay for each full year you were 41 or older

Service is capped at 20 years. A "week's pay" is your average weekly pay over the 12 weeks before your redundancy notice, up to a legal maximum.

In 2026 For redundancies on or after 6 April 2026, GOV.UK says weekly pay is capped at £751 and the most statutory redundancy pay anyone can get is £22,530. Acas says up to £30,000 of redundancy pay is tax free. These limits usually change each April.

Many employers pay more than the statutory minimum under the contract or a company scheme. Your redundancy pay is separate from notice pay, holiday pay you've built up and any wages you are owed.

Turning down another job If your employer offers you a suitable alternative job and you turn it down without good reason, GOV.UK and Acas say you may lose your right to statutory redundancy pay. Whether a job is "suitable" depends on things like pay, hours, location and status.

If something doesn't look right

The usual order is to raise it with your employer first: ask how you were selected, check the figures, and use any appeal your employer offers. If that doesn't settle it, the next step is an employment tribunal, and the first step towards one is to notify Acas for early conciliation, a free service that tries to settle the dispute.

Time limits are strict, and they changed recently. Acas says that for problems on or after 1 October 2026, most tribunal claims must be made within 6 months minus 1 day. For problems before that date it is 3 months minus 1 day for most claims. For an unfair dismissal claim the clock usually starts from the last day of your notice, or the date you were dismissed if you weren't given notice. Notifying Acas pauses the clock while conciliation runs, but Acas also says going through an appeal or grievance does not.

For statutory redundancy pay itself, GOV.UK says you have 6 months from the date your job ends to apply.

What to do next about work

Once the money and dates are clear, the bigger question is the next job. A few things tend to make the difference:

  • Decide what you're aiming for before you apply. Redundancy can be a push to do the same job elsewhere, or a chance to change direction. Applying for everything at once usually means a CV that fits nothing well.
  • Translate your experience. After years in one place, job titles and skills are often described in your old employer's language. Recruiters search in the market's language, so what you did may not be recognised as it stands.
  • Use the notice period. Time off to look for work, any outplacement support your employer offers, and the contacts you still have at work are all easier to use before you leave than after.

Getting it right for your situation

The rights are the same for everyone. The next step isn't. Someone leaving a specialist role after many years faces a very different market from a recent graduate, and the same experience can be described in ways that open doors or close them. Which direction makes sense, how ready your CV is for it, and what is most likely to hold you back all depend on your own background.

Related questions

Do I get redundancy pay if I've worked there less than two years?
Not statutory redundancy pay. GOV.UK says you normally need two years or more with your current employer. You may still be owed notice pay, holiday pay and anything your contract promises, and some employers pay redundancy to staff with less service.
Is redundancy pay taxed?
Acas says up to £30,000 of redundancy pay is tax free. Notice pay, holiday pay and wages are taxed in the usual way.
Can I be made redundant while on maternity leave?
It can happen, but you can't be chosen because you are pregnant or on maternity leave. Acas lists pregnancy and maternity among the grounds it is against the law to select someone on.
What if I'm offered another job at the same company?
If it's a suitable alternative and you refuse it without good reason, GOV.UK says you may lose statutory redundancy pay. Whether it's suitable depends on things like pay, hours, location and status.
How long do I have to challenge my redundancy?
Acas says that for problems on or after 1 October 2026, most employment tribunal claims must be made within 6 months minus 1 day, and the first step is to notify Acas for early conciliation. Going through an appeal does not pause the clock.
