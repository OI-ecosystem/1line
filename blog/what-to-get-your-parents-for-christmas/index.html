What to get your mum or dad for Christmas

They say they want nothing, they've had every obvious present, and you're still buying from an old picture of them. Here's why parents are so hard to buy for, and how to find something that feels right.

18 November 20266 min read
The short answer

Parents are hard to buy for because they usually buy what they need, tell you not to spend money, and have already had every obvious present. The gifts that tend to land are less about the object and more about what it says: that you have noticed how their life is now, not how it was when you lived at home.

That usually points to one of four things: time with you, help with something that has become a chore, something tied to the family's story, or a better version of something they use every day.

Why parents are so hard to buy for

It is a question we see again and again on gift forums and social media as Christmas gets closer, often from adults who have been buying for their parents for decades. A few things make parents a particular puzzle.

  • They tell you not to bother. “Don't waste your money on us” is close to a family tradition. It is usually meant kindly, but it leaves you with no brief at all.
  • They have had a long time to collect things. The house is full, the hobbies are well equipped, and the obvious presents have all been given before.
  • You may be buying from an old picture of them. It is easy to shop for the parent you grew up with rather than the person they are now, with new routines, new interests or a slower pace.
  • Two people, one present. Buying for “Mum and Dad” together can push you towards something bland that suits neither of them.

Working out which of these is catching you out is half the job. Each one points somewhere different.

Four kinds of gift that tend to work for parents

1. Your time, planned properly

For many parents the thing in shortest supply is time with their grown-up children. A lunch out, a trip to somewhere they used to love, or a visit with a date already fixed can mean more than anything wrapped. The planning is part of the gift: the booking made, the travel sorted, nothing left for them to organise.

2. Help with something that has become a chore

Think about the jobs they have started to put off or mention with a sigh. The garden that has got away from them, the photos stuck on an old phone, the gadget nobody has set up. Doing it for them, or arranging for it to be done, is a gift they feel for months. Be careful with the framing, though: it should feel like a treat, not a hint that they can no longer cope.

3. Something tied to the family's story

Old photos brought together, a recipe written up properly, a print of a place that matters to the family, music from the year they met. These rarely cost much and nobody else could give them. They work well next to something practical, so the present has a useful side and a sentimental one.

4. A better version of an everyday thing

Watch what they reach for every day when you visit. The worn-out slippers, the kettle that takes an age, the reading lamp that is not quite bright enough. A good replacement gets used constantly, and every use is a small reminder that you noticed.

With parents, the best present is often proof that you see who they are now, not who they were when you left home.

Where the clues usually hide

You rarely find the right idea by browsing gift guides. The clues are already there, in ordinary conversations and visits:

  • What they talk about on the phone. The new interest, the friend they have been seeing, the thing they keep meaning to sort out.
  • What they say they “used to” do. A hobby, a place or a habit they have let slip is often something they would love a reason to return to.
  • What has quietly worn out. Parents are often the last people to replace things for themselves.
  • Your brothers, sisters or their friends. They may have heard the wish you missed, and comparing notes avoids duplicate presents.
Buy for them separately in your head first Even if you end up giving one joint present, start by thinking about each parent on their own. It is much easier to find something that suits both once you know what each of them would enjoy.

What usually goes wrong

Gifts that tend to miss with parents Anything that hints they are getting old or not managing. Gadgets that need setting up and then get left in the box. More “stuff” for a house that is already full. Presents chosen from a “gifts for dad” or “gifts for mum” list, which are written for nobody in particular. And anything that is really about what you think they should be doing.

A useful test before you buy: would they still be using or remembering it in March? If the answer is probably not, look for something closer to how they actually live.

Getting it right for your mum or dad

The general picture helps you think, but it cannot tell you which of the four directions suits your parent, what to choose within your budget, or which obvious idea would quietly disappoint them. That depends on the small details only you know.

The kind of sentence that unlocks it“My mum has just retired, spends most mornings walking with a friend, always says she wants nothing and still uses the same old radio in the kitchen.”

Related questions

What if my parents say not to spend money on them?
Take it at face value on cost, not on thought. A small gift that shows you were paying attention, or some of your time, usually means more to them than anything expensive, and it respects what they asked for.
Should I buy one present for both parents or one each?
It depends on how they live. A shared gift suits parents who do most things together, such as a day out or something for the home they both use. If their interests are very different, two smaller, personal gifts tend to feel more thoughtful than one compromise.
Is it a good idea to club together with my brothers and sisters?
Often, yes. A joint gift lets you aim for something none of you would manage alone, and it avoids three of you turning up with the same thing. Agree early who is organising it and roughly what everyone is putting in.
Should I include a gift receipt?
It is a kind thing to do. In England, Citizens Advice explains there is no automatic right to return something from a shop just because you have changed your mind; it depends on the shop's own returns policy. Online orders usually come with a 14-day cooling-off period, but personalised or custom-made items do not.
My parents live far away. What works?
Things that arrive well and keep you in touch: something they will use up, a photo or memory gift, or a plan for a visit with the date already in it. A regular call or a shared project you can both work on can mean more than the parcel.
