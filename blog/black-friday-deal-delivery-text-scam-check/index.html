How can I tell if a Black Friday deal, delivery text or 'order problem' email is a scam?

The weeks around Black Friday bring real bargains, and a wave of messages built to look like them. Most scams lean on the same few tricks. Knowing them, and knowing how to check without using the message itself, does most of the work.

17 November 20266 min read
The short answer

Treat any unexpected deal, delivery text or order email as unproven until you've checked it yourself. The common warning signs are pressure to act fast, a price that seems too good to be true, a request to pay in an unusual way such as a bank transfer, and a link you're asked to click. Don't use the link or number in the message: go to the company's official website or app directly. If you think you've been caught out, contact your bank straight away and report it.

Why scams spike around Black Friday

It's a question we see a lot on UK consumer forums at this time of year. Someone has a text saying a parcel can't be delivered, an email saying there's a problem with an order, or an advert for a big-brand product at a fraction of the usual price, and they can't tell whether it's real.

That confusion is the point. In late November many of us are expecting deliveries, signing up to newsletters and clicking on offers we'd normally ignore. A fake message only has to look like one of the many real ones you're already getting.

The NCSC notes that criminals put harmful links into text messages, emails and, increasingly, social media posts, and that the aim of a scam text is often simply to get you to click. The link can lead to a site that installs malware or collects your passwords and personal details.

In 2026, Black Friday falls on Friday 27 November. Offers now run for weeks either side of it, so the same caution applies from early November right through to Christmas deliveries.

The warning signs that matter most

Scams come in many forms, but official guidance keeps returning to the same few signs. One on its own doesn't prove a message is fake. Several together should make you stop.

  • It came out of the blue. Citizens Advice says a scam often starts when someone you don't know contacts you unexpectedly.
  • It's too good to be true. A deep discount on something that's rarely discounted is the oldest hook. The NCSC specifically warns about promotions that seem too good to be true and "unbelievable offers".
  • It's urgent. "Pay today or your parcel will be returned." "Your account will be closed." Citizens Advice says that even if a message says it's urgent, you should still check before paying.
  • It asks you to pay in an unusual way. Citizens Advice lists being asked to transfer money quickly, or to pay by gift vouchers or money transfer services, as signs of a scam. The NCSC's advice is never to pay by direct bank transfer when shopping online.
  • It wants passwords, PINs or card details. Citizens Advice lists being asked for passwords or PINs as a warning sign.
  • The web address is slightly off. The NCSC gives the example of a made-up address that adds words to a real shop's name. Fake sites often look polished; the address is where they slip.
The padlock isn't proof Citizens Advice says not to rely on the padlock in your browser's address bar: it doesn't guarantee you're dealing with a real company. Fake shops can have one too.

Deals, delivery texts and order emails: how each one usually works

The three most common Black Friday messages work in slightly different ways, which changes what to look for.

Type of messageWhat it usually wantsThe safer way to check
A deal advert or websiteYour payment for goods that never arrive, or your card detailsLook the shop up independently, check its contact details, terms and reviews, and pay by card
A delivery or "missed parcel" textA click on a link, often to pay a small "fee" and hand over card detailsTrack any parcel you're expecting through the retailer's or courier's own website or app
An "order problem" or "account issue" emailYour login details, or a payment to "fix" the orderSign in to your account by typing the address yourself, and see if anything is actually wrong

The common thread is the route. A genuine problem will still be there when you go to the company's website or app directly. The NCSC suggests typing the official website address into your browser rather than following a link.

Checking without using the message

The safest checks use nothing from the message itself: not the link, not the phone number, not the "reply to confirm" option.

  1. Pause. A real offer or delivery will survive a few minutes of checking. Pressure to act now is a reason to slow down.
  2. Find the company yourself. Use its official website, its app or the number on your statement or card. Citizens Advice suggests checking a company's details, such as its postal address, and looking for independent reviews.
  3. Phone calls: hang up and wait. Citizens Advice suggests hanging up, waiting five minutes and calling back on a number from the official website. If it's about your bank, it suggests calling 159, a secure service that connects you directly with your bank.
  4. Pay in a way that protects you. The NCSC suggests using a credit card if you have one, and says debit cards offer less protection, though a refund may be possible through chargeback.
Report it even if you didn't fall for it The NCSC says most phone providers let you forward a suspicious text to 7726 for free, so they can trace and block the sender. Suspicious emails can be reported to the NCSC's Suspicious Email Reporting Service.

If you've already clicked, paid or given details

Act quickly, and don't let embarrassment slow you down. These scams are built to catch careful people.

  • Tell your bank straight away if you've paid or shared card or account details. The NCSC advises always contacting your bank directly using its official website or phone number.
  • Report it. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, Report Fraud has replaced Action Fraud: you can report at reportfraud.police.uk or on 0300 123 2040. In Scotland, report it to Police Scotland on 101.
  • Keep the evidence: the message, the web address, any receipts and the dates. Which? notes that banks may need a crime reference number to process a reimbursement claim, which is another reason to report.
  • Stay wary afterwards. Treat any later contact about the same order, parcel or payment with the same caution, and check it the same way: through official contact details you find yourself.

Getting it right for your situation

Warning signs are general. The message in front of you is specific: a particular sender, a particular link, a particular wording that may match something being reported right now. And if you've already acted, what to do next depends on exactly what you shared, how you paid and who you need to tell.

Related questions

Does a padlock in the address bar mean a shopping site is safe?
No. Citizens Advice says not to rely on seeing a padlock in your browser's address bar, because it doesn't guarantee you're buying from a real company. It suggests checking the company's details, its terms and conditions and independent reviews as well.
How do I report a scam text?
The NCSC says most phone providers are part of a scheme that lets you forward a suspicious text to 7726 for free. Your provider can then look into where it came from and block the sender. If 7726 doesn't work, contact your phone provider.
Where do I report a scam if I've lost money?
In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, report it to Report Fraud, the service that replaced Action Fraud, at reportfraud.police.uk or on 0300 123 2040. In Scotland, report it to Police Scotland on 101. Tell your bank as well, using its official number.
What is 159?
Citizens Advice describes 159 as a secure service that connects you directly with your bank. If you get a call you're unsure about, it suggests hanging up and calling 159, rather than calling back a number the caller gives you.
Is it safer to pay by credit card when shopping online?
The NCSC advises using a credit card for online payments if you have one, as many protect purchases under the Consumer Credit Act. It says debit cards offer less protection, though you may be able to get a refund through chargeback, and it advises never paying by direct bank transfer.
