My flight was cancelled and the airline still hasn't refunded me. What can I do?

The flight was cancelled, the refund was promised, and weeks later the money still isn't back. A stalled refund is one of the most common airline complaints, and there are clear rules and routes for it.

19 November 20266 min read
The short answer

If your flight is covered by UK rules and it was cancelled, the airline must let you choose a refund or an alternative flight, and the CAA says a refund should be paid within 7 days. If it isn't, put a clear written complaint to the airline. If eight weeks pass without a proper answer, you can escalate to the airline's dispute scheme or the CAA, and depending on how you paid, your card provider may also be able to help.

Why refunds stall

It's a question we see often on UK travel and money forums. The flight is cancelled or rescheduled, the airline sends an email saying a refund is "being processed", and then nothing. Some people are told it was approved months ago. Others find their refund request has turned into a compensation claim, or a voucher, or a booking they never asked for.

Most delays come down to a handful of things: the request went through the wrong channel, the airline treated it as a different kind of claim, the booking was made through a third party, or nobody ever put a clear, dated request in writing. None of that changes what you're owed, but each one can slow things down.

What the rules say about refunds

For flights covered by UK law, the CAA says that when a flight is cancelled your airline must let you choose between two options: a refund, or an alternative flight. If you still want to travel, the airline must find you another flight, and it's up to you whether that's as soon as possible or at a later date that suits you.

On timing, the CAA's current travel advice says the airline should provide a refund within 7 days. It also says that if you booked through a third party, the refund may take longer.

Refunds aren't only for cancellations. The CAA says that if your flight is delayed by at least five hours, you can choose not to travel and get a refund for the flights on your booking that you haven't yet taken. If you were on a connecting journey and the delay is more than five hours, you can choose a refund for the unused part of your ticket and abandon the journey.

Choose carefully The CAA warns that you should not choose a refund if you still wish to travel.

Refunds, compensation and vouchers are different things

A lot of stalled refunds come from mixing these up.

  • A refund is your money back for the flights you didn't take.
  • Compensation is a separate fixed sum that may be owed for a late cancellation or long delay, and it can be refused if the airline shows extraordinary circumstances. Our article on claiming flight compensation covers it.
  • A voucher or credit isn't one of the two options the CAA lists for a cancellation. If you've been given one and wanted cash, it's worth being clear in writing which option you chose.

If you asked for both a refund and compensation, keep them as two clearly labelled requests. A single message asking for "my money back" can be handled as just one of them.

Booked through an agent or as a package?

Who owes you the money depends on who you paid.

  • Direct with the airline: the airline handles the refund, and the CAA's timings above apply.
  • Through a travel agent or booking site: the money often has to pass from the airline to the agent and then to you, which is one reason the CAA says third-party refunds may take longer. Ask each side, in writing, where the refund is.
  • As part of a package holiday: different rules apply, and you deal with the holiday organiser rather than the airline. The government's guidance on package holiday refunds explains your rights there.

If the airline doesn't pay, or doesn't reply

The usual route goes in stages:

  1. Put it in writing to the airline. A clear, dated request for the refund, with your booking reference, the flight and what you chose. Chat messages and phone calls are easy to lose.
  2. Wait up to eight weeks for a proper answer. The CAA says that if your airline takes more than eight weeks to respond, you can escalate.
  3. Escalate. If the airline is signed up to an alternative dispute resolution (ADR) scheme, you take it there. If it isn't, the CAA says its own Passenger Advice and Complaints Team (PACT) can look at it.

Depending on how you paid, there may also be a route through your card provider.

  • Section 75: Citizens Advice says that if you paid by credit card for a single item costing more than £100 and no more than £30,000, the card provider can be jointly responsible under the Consumer Credit Act. You usually can't use it if you paid through a third party such as a travel agent.
  • Chargeback: if Section 75 doesn't apply, you can ask your debit or credit card provider for a chargeback. It isn't guaranteed, and the provider might say no.
  • PayPal: Citizens Advice says you have 180 days from when you paid to open a dispute.

What usually goes wrong

  • No clear start date. Without one written request, it's hard to show when the eight weeks began.
  • The refund and the compensation are tangled. One gets refused and the other quietly drops with it.
  • A voucher is accepted by accident. Clicking through a rebooking or credit option can be treated as your choice.
  • The money is stuck between airline and agent. Each says the other has it, and nobody is chased in writing.
  • Card routes are left too late. PayPal, for example, has a time limit for opening a dispute, so it's worth checking your options early.

Getting it right for your situation

The general rules are clear. Applying them to one booking is where it gets harder: which rule covers your flight, whether you're owed a refund, compensation or both, who actually holds your money, what you can show, and which escalation route fits your airline and the way you paid. A request that gets those right, with a clear timeline and a sensible deadline, is much harder to leave sitting in a queue.

Related questions

How long does an airline have to refund a cancelled flight?
The CAA says the airline should provide the refund within 7 days. It adds that if you booked through a third party, the refund may take longer.
Can I get a refund if my flight was delayed rather than cancelled?
The CAA says that if your flight is delayed by at least five hours, you can choose not to travel and get a refund for the flights you have not yet taken on your booking. You should not choose this if you still want to travel.
Do I have to accept a voucher instead of my money back?
When a flight covered by UK rules is cancelled, the CAA describes two options: a refund or an alternative flight. A voucher is not one of the two options it lists, so it is worth being clear in writing which option you chose.
I booked through a travel agent. Who owes me the refund?
The CAA says refunds can take longer when you booked through a third party. Citizens Advice also notes that you usually can't use Section 75 if you paid a travel agent rather than the trader directly, though you can still ask your card provider about chargeback.
Can my credit card company refund me instead?
Possibly. Citizens Advice says Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act can apply if you paid by credit card for a single item costing more than £100 and no more than £30,000. If Section 75 doesn't apply, you can ask your card provider for a chargeback, though it might say no.
