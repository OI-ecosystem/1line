My takeaway delivery app refused a refund. What can I do?

Food that never turned up, a bag with half the order missing, or a meal you couldn’t eat, and the app says no. A refusal in a chat window isn’t always the last word. What matters is what went wrong, who you bought from and how you paid.

13 November 20266 min read
The short answer

When a delivery app refuses a refund, that’s its own process talking, not the end of your rights. Food bought from a business is still goods, and if it never arrived, was missing, or wasn’t what you ordered, the law can still be on your side. If the app won’t move, how you paid and a short written complaint usually decide what happens next.

Why the app said no

It’s one of the complaints we see most often on UK social media at the moment: an order arrives cold, soaked, missing items or not at all, and a refund request is turned down, sometimes in seconds. The reasons given vary. The driver’s GPS says the food was delivered. The photo “doesn’t show the problem”. The account has too many past claims. A partial refund is offered that covers a fraction of the order.

Most of these refusals come from an automated or scripted process designed to handle huge numbers of small claims quickly. That process decides what the company is willing to pay without being pushed. It doesn’t decide what you’re entitled to. Keeping those two questions apart is the first step.

Food is still goods

A takeaway is a purchase from a business, and the Consumer Rights Act 2015 covers goods bought from a trader. If what turned up didn’t meet the contract, you may be able to reject it. Under section 22 of the Act the short-term right to reject normally lasts 30 days, but for goods that can reasonably be expected to perish sooner, it ends when that shorter period ends. For a hot meal that means acting straight away, not the next morning.

It helps to name the problem plainly, because each is argued differently:

  • It never arrived. Citizens Advice says that when you buy from a business, making sure the item reaches you is the seller’s responsibility, and chasing a courier isn’t your job.
  • Items were missing, or it was someone else’s order. You paid for food you didn’t get.
  • It arrived spoiled, spilled or contaminated. Food that can’t reasonably be eaten is a different complaint from food that was just disappointing.
  • You were charged for an order that was cancelled. If nothing was supplied, the question is why you paid at all.
Where your rights don’t reach Changing your mind is different. Citizens Advice says there is no cooling-off period for things that deteriorate quickly, like food. Rights about missing, wrong or undelivered orders are a separate matter.

Who you actually bought from

On delivery apps this is often unclear. Sometimes the app sells the food to you; sometimes it acts for the restaurant, which is the seller, and the app only takes the order and arranges delivery. Who delivered it can be a third business again. The order confirmation and the app’s terms usually say which, and that decides who your complaint should be aimed at.

In practice the app usually controls refunds either way, so it’s normally the first place to go. But if it says “that’s the restaurant’s problem”, or the restaurant says “take it up with the app”, knowing who the seller is stops you being passed back and forth.

If you found something in your food Keep the food and packaging, and take photos before you throw anything away. The Food Standards Agency points people to their local council’s food safety team to report a food safety or hygiene problem. That report is about hygiene; getting your money back is a separate complaint.

How you paid can open a second route

If the app won’t move, the way you paid may give you another way to get your money back.

How you paidWhat may be available
Debit card or charge cardA chargeback request through your bank. Citizens Advice gives no minimum amount for this.
Credit cardA chargeback, or Section 75 if you paid more than £100 and no more than £30,000 for a single item (rare for a takeaway)
PayPalA PayPal dispute, which Citizens Advice says you can open within 180 days of paying

Two cautions. Citizens Advice notes that the trader can challenge a chargeback, so your evidence still matters. And if your bank says it won’t take a chargeback further, you can complain to the Financial Ombudsman Service.

The last resort is a county court claim for money. GOV.UK says you can claim money you’re owed by a person or business, apply online or by post, and that the court may organise free mediation depending on the amount. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own processes. For one small order, few people go this far, but it’s worth knowing the route exists.

What usually goes wrong

  • The evidence went in the bin. Photos of the bag, the seal, the receipt stapled to it and the food itself are often all you have.
  • Everything stayed in the app chat. Chat windows close and transcripts disappear. Save screenshots with dates and times.
  • A partial refund was accepted without thinking. Taking a small credit may make it harder to argue for the rest later.
  • “Claims history” was treated as final. It’s the company’s own fraud rule. It may matter, but it doesn’t remove your rights for an order that genuinely went wrong.
  • Waiting too long. Food perishes, memories fade and card routes have time limits.

Getting it right for your situation

The general picture is the easy part. The hard part is one real order: which problem you actually have, who the seller was, whether your photos and screenshots are enough, what to put in writing so it isn’t brushed off by another automated reply, and which route to take if the answer is still no. A clear complaint that names the right problem and the right party tends to be taken more seriously than a string of angry chat messages.

Related questions

Can I get a refund on a takeaway if I just changed my mind?
Usually not. Citizens Advice says there is no cooling-off period for things that deteriorate quickly, like food. That is different from an order that was missing, wrong or never delivered, where your other rights still apply.
Who is responsible if my takeaway never arrived?
Citizens Advice says that when you buy from a business, it is the seller’s responsibility to make sure the item is delivered to you, and chasing a courier is not your job. Check your order confirmation to see who the seller is: the app or the restaurant.
Can I use a chargeback for a small takeaway order?
Citizens Advice says you can ask your bank for a chargeback if you paid by debit card or charge card, and it gives no minimum amount. The trader can challenge it, and if your bank refuses to pursue it you can complain to the Financial Ombudsman Service.
Does Section 75 cover takeaway orders?
Rarely. Citizens Advice says Section 75 applies when you paid by credit card more than £100 and no more than £30,000 for a single item, so most takeaway orders fall below it.
What if I found something in my food?
Keep the food and packaging and take photos. The Food Standards Agency points people to their local council’s food safety team to report a food safety or hygiene problem. Treat that report as separate from getting your money back.
