Bills & money

My energy supplier wants to raise my direct debit, but I’m in credit. Can they?

A credit balance and a bigger monthly payment can look like a contradiction. Often there’s a reason behind it, but the supplier has to be able to show it, and you can push back if the numbers don’t add up.

14 November 20266 min read
The short answer

Yes, they can, but they have to be able to justify it. A fixed direct debit is based on an estimate of your use over a whole year, so credit built up in summer is often needed for winter. Your supplier must explain how it worked out the new amount and show the readings it used, and you can ask it to lower the payment or refund credit if the figures don’t add up.

Why a supplier raises a direct debit when you’re in credit

It’s a question we see often on UK money forums and social media, especially as the weather turns. Someone checks their account, sees a healthy credit balance, and then gets a message saying their monthly payment is going up. It feels like being charged more for money you’ve already paid.

The reason is usually in how direct debits work. Citizens Advice explains that a fixed direct debit is normally based on an estimate of the energy you’ll use over a whole year. The monthly amount is spread evenly, but your use isn’t. Most homes use far more gas and electricity in winter than in summer.

That means credit tends to build up in the warmer months and then get used up over winter. A balance that looks large in October may be needed by February. If the supplier’s forecast says your credit won’t cover the rest of the year, it may raise the payment even though the account is in credit today.

Ofgem has also pointed to other things that can drive a big adjustment: a change of tariff or price, a debit balance building up, or recent meter readings that show higher use than expected.

Look at the whole year, not today’s balance The question isn’t “am I in credit now?” but “will what I pay over the next twelve months roughly match what I use?” That’s the test the supplier’s figure should be meeting.

What your supplier has to do

Suppliers can’t simply pick a number. Ofgem says it is for suppliers to make sure direct debits are set correctly, based on all the relevant information they have, and to explain any changes in a way that helps customers understand their payments. In 2022 Ofgem strengthened the rules so that suppliers must set fixed direct debits using the best and most current information available to them.

Citizens Advice sets out what that means for you in practice:

  • You can ask how the figure was worked out. The supplier must explain clearly how it reached the new amount and give you the meter readings it used.
  • You can ask for it to be lowered. If you’re not happy with the calculation, you can ask the supplier to set a payment that more accurately reflects your use.
  • You should be told first. Under the Direct Debit Guarantee, your supplier has to tell you about an increase before it happens. Citizens Advice says the notice period might be between 3 and 10 days, depending on the supplier.

Citizens Advice also says that if you used the energy more than 12 months ago, your supplier usually can’t charge you for it. That matters if a rise is linked to a catch-up bill for older usage.

When the figures don’t add up

Some increases are fair. Others come from out-of-date information. The most common problem is old or estimated readings. If the supplier is working from an estimate that’s too high, its forecast of your year will be too high too.

That’s why Citizens Advice suggests sending regular meter readings, monthly if you can, so the supplier can set the right level. When you ask for the readings it used, comparing them with your own meter is often the quickest way to spot where a calculation has gone wrong.

Other things worth knowing:

  • Price changes feed through. If the unit rate or standing charge on your tariff has changed, the forecast for the year changes with it, even if your use hasn’t.
  • Debt and credit are both part of the sum. Citizens Advice gives the example of a payment raised to clear arrears and then reduced again once the debt is repaid. Credit works the other way and should reduce what’s needed.
  • Fixed isn’t the only option. Citizens Advice says you might be able to set up variable direct debit payments, which follow what you actually use. If your supplier doesn’t offer them, switching is an option.
Struggling to pay? If a higher payment isn’t affordable, Citizens Advice says to talk to your supplier about a payment plan rather than letting it build up.

Getting your credit back

The other side of the question is the credit itself. Citizens Advice says you can claim credit back at any time. You contact the supplier, say how much you want refunded and give an up-to-date meter reading.

Citizens Advice says suppliers might refund money owed at the end of the year, or reduce your direct debit instead. If they don’t, you can ask. A supplier can say no if it has a good reason, for example if the balance is small and it’s summer, but it must explain its decision.

There’s a balance to strike. Citizens Advice suggests leaving credit on the account over summer and autumn to help with higher winter costs, and claiming back only the amount above your monthly direct debit. Taking all the credit out in autumn can be the very thing that triggers a higher payment later.

If you still disagree

If the supplier won’t explain the figure, or won’t lower a payment you think is too high, Citizens Advice says to make a formal complaint to the supplier. Make it clear it’s a complaint rather than a query, and keep a note of what you’re told and when.

Energy complaints that the supplier doesn’t resolve can go on to an independent ombudsman. We cover how that route works, and when you can use it, in our article on what to do when your energy bill is wrong.

Getting it right for your situation

The rules are clear enough. The hard part is knowing whether they point your way. Is your new payment a fair forecast of the year ahead, or is it built on old estimates? Is your credit a buffer you’ll need for winter, or money you should ask for back? And is this bill the one to deal with first, or is something else in your household budget more pressing?

Related questions

Can my energy supplier increase my direct debit without telling me?
No. Citizens Advice says your supplier has to let you know about a payment increase before it happens, under the Direct Debit Guarantee. The notice period depends on the supplier and might be between 3 and 10 days.
How do I find out how my new direct debit was worked out?
Ask your supplier. Citizens Advice says it must explain clearly how it reached the new figure and give you the meter readings it used, so you can check them against your bill.
Can I ask for my energy credit back?
Yes. Citizens Advice says you can claim credit at any time by telling the supplier how much you want back and giving an up-to-date meter reading. The supplier can refuse if it has a good reason, but it must explain its decision.
Should I keep some credit on my account for winter?
Citizens Advice suggests leaving credit on the account over the summer and autumn to help cover higher winter costs, and claiming back only the amount above your monthly direct debit.
What if my supplier won't lower my direct debit?
Citizens Advice says to make a formal complaint to the supplier. It also says you might be able to switch to variable direct debit payments, or move to a supplier that offers them.
