Complaints & scams

My complaint has been ignored, or I've had a deadlock letter. What next?

Weeks of chasing and no answer, or a letter saying the company can do no more. Either way, it usually isn't the end of the road. Most sectors have a clock, an independent body at the end of it, and a deadline you need to keep.

16 November 20266 min read
The short answer

If a company hasn't resolved your complaint within its set period (usually 8 weeks, but 6 weeks for phone and broadband, and 40 working days for rail), or it sends you a deadlock letter, you can normally take the complaint to the ombudsman for that sector. Ombudsman schemes are free. Each one has a time limit for bringing your complaint, so don't leave a final response sitting in a drawer.

Why complaints stall

It's one of the questions we see most often on UK consumer forums and social media. A complaint goes in, then nothing: no reply, or a string of chat transcripts that go round in circles. Other people get a reply, but it's a letter saying the company has looked into it and won't change its position.

Both feel like a dead end, but they're often the point where the complaint can finally move somewhere else.

Citizens Advice puts it this way: an ombudsman will investigate if the organisation takes too long to resolve your complaint. That's usually 8 weeks, but the period varies, so it's worth checking the scheme you'll be using.

What a deadlock letter means

A deadlock letter is the company's final response: it sets out its decision and says it can't do any more. It doesn't always arrive as a letter. The Rail Ombudsman notes it might be an email, or even a text.

Two things are worth knowing:

  • It can let you escalate early. The Energy Ombudsman, for example, says a supplier has 8 weeks to resolve a dispute unless you receive a deadlock letter, which lets you come to it sooner.
  • You don't always need one. Citizens Advice says you don't need a deadlock letter if the organisation is simply taking too long. Once the waiting period is up, you can usually go ahead anyway.
Read the final response carefully A proper final response normally tells you how to take the complaint further. The Financial Ombudsman says a final response from a financial business should mention your right to refer the complaint to it within the next 6 months.

How long they have, sector by sector

The waiting periods aren't the same everywhere, and some have changed recently. Here's how the main ones compare, from each body's own guidance:

The phone and broadband change catches many people out. Ofcom reduced the wait from eight weeks to six, and it says providers must send you a letter telling you about your right to use their ADR scheme if a complaint hasn't been resolved in that time. If you're still waiting at week seven for a provider to "escalate internally", the next step may already be open to you.

Watch the deadline The waiting period protects the business; the time limit protects the process. Miss it and the ombudsman may not be able to look at your case, except in exceptional circumstances.

What an ombudsman can and can't do

Citizens Advice describes ombudsman schemes as independent, free and impartial. They look at both sides and decide what's fair. For private companies, the decision could be legally binding: the Energy Ombudsman, for example, says that once you accept its decision, the supplier must carry it out within 28 days. If you don't accept it, you're usually free to take the matter further another way.

There are limits. Citizens Advice says an ombudsman won't investigate if you've already started court action. Some schemes cap what they can award: the Rail Ombudsman says it can't look into complaints where you're claiming more than £2,500 in compensation. And an ombudsman will usually expect you to have given the business a fair chance to put things right first.

If there's no ombudsman

Not every business sits under an ombudsman. For shops and other sellers, Citizens Advice points to a few routes:

  • Alternative dispute resolution (ADR). Some sellers belong to an ADR scheme. If yours doesn't, you can ask whether it would be willing to use one. Citizens Advice says an ADR scheme has to deal with your case within 90 days.
  • Your card or payment provider. If you paid by card, PayPal or buy now pay later, you may be able to ask the provider for help. If the provider doesn't respond within 8 weeks, you can complain to the Financial Ombudsman, whether or not the seller has an ADR scheme.
  • Court. You can make a claim to the court if the problem still isn't resolved, sometimes called a small claim. A judge will usually expect you to have tried ADR first, and Citizens Advice suggests it's probably only worth it if the amount is significant.

What usually goes wrong

  • The complaint was never formally logged. Calls and chats about a problem aren't always treated as a complaint, so the clock may not have started.
  • No record of dates. Every rule here turns on dates: when you complained, when they replied, when the final response arrived.
  • Going to the wrong body. Each sector has its own scheme, and some businesses belong to one of two. Sending a case to the wrong one costs weeks.
  • Waiting too long after the final response. Time limits like 6 or 12 months pass quickly when you're worn out from chasing.
  • A muddled case. An ombudsman reads what you send. A clear account of what happened, what you've already tried and what you want tends to get further than a long, angry history.

Getting it right for your situation

The general rules are the easy part. The hard part is applying them to your own complaint: which body covers it, whether the clock has actually run out, whether you're inside the time limit, what evidence you need, and what you should be asking for. Get one of those wrong and you can lose weeks, or the right to escalate at all.

Related questions

What is a deadlock letter?
It's the company's final response to your complaint, saying it can't do any more. The Rail Ombudsman notes it can come as a letter, an email or even a text. Once you have one, you can usually take the complaint to the ombudsman for that sector without waiting for the full response period to run out.
Do I need a deadlock letter to go to an ombudsman?
No. Citizens Advice says you don't need one if the organisation is taking too long. Once the response period for that sector has passed, you can usually go to the ombudsman anyway.
How long does a phone or broadband provider have to deal with my complaint?
Ofcom cut the period from eight weeks to six, from 8 April 2026. After six weeks, if the complaint isn't resolved, providers must tell you about your right to use their ADR scheme: the Communications Ombudsman or CISAS.
Does it cost anything to use an ombudsman?
Citizens Advice says ombudsman schemes are independent, free and impartial. The Energy Ombudsman and the Rail Ombudsman both say their service is free for consumers.
What if there's no ombudsman for my complaint?
Citizens Advice says some sellers belong to an alternative dispute resolution (ADR) scheme, and you can ask a seller that doesn't whether it will use one. If the problem still isn't resolved, you can make a claim to the court, sometimes called a small claim. A judge will usually expect you to have tried ADR first.
