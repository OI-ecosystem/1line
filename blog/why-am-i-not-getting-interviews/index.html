Work & words

Why am I not getting any interviews after hundreds of applications?

If you've sent application after application and heard nothing but silence or automated rejections, it's easy to assume the problem is you. More often, it's how your experience is being presented, and that can be changed.

7 November 20265 min read
The short answer

When lots of applications get no response at all, the CV is usually the first place to look. The most common causes are a CV that doesn't clearly match the role it's sent to, achievements described as duties rather than results, and a top section that doesn't say who you are and what you're aiming for.

Sending more applications rarely fixes this. Sending better-matched ones usually does.

Silence is a signal, not a verdict

It's one of the questions we see most often on careers forums: someone with a degree, or years of solid experience, has applied for dozens or hundreds of jobs and got almost nothing back. It's demoralising, and it's very easy to read it as a judgement on your worth.

It's more useful to read it as information. Where you're getting stuck tells you what to fix:

  • No responses at all usually points to the CV and how well it matches the roles you're applying for.
  • First-stage calls that go nowhere can point to a mismatch between what the CV promises and what comes across in conversation.
  • Interviews but no offers usually means the CV is working, and the effort belongs in interview preparation.

This article focuses on the first of these, because it's the most common and the most fixable.

The most common reasons a CV gets passed over

1. It reads as a list of duties

Many CVs describe what the job involved rather than what the person achieved. "Responsible for managing the team rota" tells a reader what the role was. It doesn't tell them whether you were good at it. Results, scale and outcomes are what make one candidate stand out from another with the same job title.

2. One CV is doing every job

A single, general CV sent to very different roles tends to fit all of them loosely and none of them well. Readers, and the screening software many employers use, look for a clear match between what the advert asks for and what the CV shows. If that match has to be worked out, it often isn't.

3. The top third doesn't land

Whoever reads your CV may decide quickly whether to keep going. If the opening section is vague, generic or missing, they have to dig to find out who you are and what you want. Most won't. The top of the page needs to say, plainly, what you do and what you're aiming for.

4. Your strongest material is buried

The achievement most relevant to the job is often sitting halfway down page two, under an older role. Order matters. What a reader sees first shapes how they read everything else.

5. It's written for the job you had, not the one you want

This is especially common for career changers and people returning to work. The experience is relevant, but it's described in the language of the old field, so the connection to the new role isn't obvious to anyone reading it.

A CV isn't a record of everything you've done. It's an argument for why you fit this particular job.

What more applications won't fix

The volume trap When nothing is working, the natural response is to apply for more jobs, faster. But if the same CV goes to every role, a hundred more applications tend to produce the same result. It also leaves less time to make any single application strong.

It's also worth being honest about factors a CV can't change. Some fields are crowded at the moment, some roles attract very large numbers of applicants, and some adverts are filled internally. None of that is a reason to give up, but it is a reason to judge yourself less harshly while you work on the parts you can control.

Where to look first

Before rewriting anything, it helps to take a step back and look at your CV the way a stranger would. A few questions to ask yourself:

  • Could someone tell, from the top section alone, what job I'm going for?
  • Do my bullet points show results, or just responsibilities?
  • Is the experience most relevant to this role near the top?
  • Am I using the same words for my skills that the adverts use?
  • Would this CV make sense to someone outside my current or previous field?
Get a second pair of eyes It's hard to judge your own CV. A friend in a similar field, a former manager, or a careers adviser can often spot in minutes what you've stopped seeing.

Getting it right for your experience

Knowing the common problems is the easy part. The hard part is applying them to your own history: which achievements to lead with, how to describe them so their value is obvious, and how to angle the whole document towards one specific target role. That depends on what you've actually done, and on the job you're going for.

Related questions

Should I send the same CV to every job?
Usually not. Your core history stays the same, but the summary and the order of your achievements should shift to match what each role asks for most. A CV that fits one job well tends to do better than one that fits every job loosely.
Is it better to apply to fewer jobs?
Often, yes. Fewer, better-matched applications with a tailored CV tend to get more responses than a high volume of near-identical ones. The time saved can go into making each one fit.
Do I need a cover letter?
If the advert asks for one, always. Even when it's optional, a short letter that links your experience to the role can help, especially if you're changing direction or have a gap to explain.
How do I explain a gap in my CV?
Briefly and honestly. One line is usually enough. If you did anything useful in that time, such as learning, caring or project work, mention it. What matters most is that the rest of the CV shows you're ready for the role now.
I get interviews but no offers. Is that a CV problem?
Probably not. If your CV is getting you into the room, it's doing its job. The gap is more likely in interview preparation, how you're describing your experience out loud, or how closely the roles match what you want.
