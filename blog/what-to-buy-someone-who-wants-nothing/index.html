What to buy someone who says they don't want anything

“I don't need anything” is rarely the end of the conversation. It's usually a clue. Here's how to read it, and how to find a present they'll actually be glad of.

7 November 20264 min read
The short answer

Treat “nothing” as information, not instructions. People who say it usually mean one of three things: they don't want more stuff, they don't want you spending on them, or they can't think of anything on the spot.

Match the gift to which one it is: something they'll use up, time or an experience, a better version of something they use every day, or a small thing that proves you were listening.

What “I don't want anything” usually means

Almost nobody genuinely wants nothing. What they're telling you is narrower than that, and it's worth working out which version you're dealing with.

  • “I don't want more things.” Their cupboards are full and they don't want another object to find a home for. This is the most common meaning, especially as people get older.
  • “Please don't spend money on me.” Often modesty, or a kindness towards you. What they'd value is thought, not cost.
  • “I can't think of anything right now.” They've been put on the spot. The want exists; they just haven't named it.

The quickest way to tell which is to look at how they live rather than what they say. Someone who keeps everything tidy and spare is probably the first. Someone who always insists on paying is probably the second.

Four kinds of gift that work

1. Something they'll use up

Good coffee or tea, a favourite treat, proper bath or skincare things, a refill of something they already love. It gets enjoyed and then it's gone, so there's nothing to store and no guilt.

2. Time, or an experience

Tickets, a class, a meal out, a day trip. Often better still: an experience with you. For people who say “don't spend money,” a promise of something you'll do for them lands well: a cooked dinner, a sorted-out garden, a lift to the airport without the usual negotiation.

3. A better version of an everyday thing

Look at what they use every single day and what's quietly worn out. The chipped mug. The slippers held together by habit. The umbrella that turns inside out. The charger that only works at an angle. A better version of something they already rely on is never clutter.

4. Proof you were listening

The book they mentioned once in passing. The sweets from their childhood. The print of a place that matters to them. These cost the least and mean the most, because the real gift is that you remembered.

The real gift is rarely the object. It's the evidence that you've been paying attention.

Where the clues usually are

You rarely find the right present by browsing. You find it by noticing. The clues tend to sit in three places:

  • Their routines. What they do every day, and what they grumble about while doing it.
  • The things they use constantly. Especially anything worn, broken or quietly annoying.
  • What they've said in passing. “I keep meaning to…”, “I miss…” and “I'd love to try…” are worth remembering.

Someone close to them, such as a partner, sibling or best friend, often knows more than you think.

What to avoid

Gifts that tend to miss Novelty items that get a laugh and then a drawer. “Self-improvement” presents that send a message nobody asked for. Anything large that needs space they don't have. And anything with an ongoing cost, unless they've asked for it.

Turning it into ideas for one person

Knowing the four kinds of gift is the easy part. The hard part is applying it to one specific person: which kind suits them, what exactly to buy, and which obvious choices would miss.

Related questions

Is it rude to buy a present when someone has said not to?
Usually not, as long as you respect what they meant. Keep it small and personal rather than lavish. If you both genuinely prefer no presents, agree it openly so neither of you is caught out.
Is a gift card a good idea for someone who wants nothing?
It can be, if it's for a specific place they already love, such as their favourite café, bookshop or garden centre. A general-purpose card can feel impersonal, so add a short note saying why you chose it.
What if they really do mean nothing?
Then give something that isn't a thing: time together, a job done for them, a handwritten letter, or a donation in their name to a cause they care about.
How early should I start thinking about it?
As early as you can. Experiences often need booking, and the best ideas come from things people mention in passing, so keep a running note on your phone through the year.
