Is January a good time to look for a new job?

The new year is when a lot of people decide it's time to move on. Whether January works for you depends less on the calendar and more on what you're moving towards, and on getting the practical side of leaving right.

27 November 20266 min read
The short answer

January can be a good time to look, because many employers set new budgets and hiring plans and many people decide to move after the break. But a busy month also means more competition. What usually decides how a January search goes is how clear you are about the kind of role you want, and whether you avoid the practical mistakes people make when they resign, such as handing in notice before an offer is firm.

Why January feels like the moment

It's a question we see often on UK job and career forums at this time of year: someone has had enough of their current role, or has been applying for months without much luck, and wonders whether to wait for the new year and go again. For many people the break is the first real chance to step back and think about work.

There are some sound reasons January can work well:

  • Budgets reset. Many employers plan hiring for the year ahead, and roles that were on hold before Christmas are often advertised once the new year starts.
  • You have time to think. A few days away from work makes it easier to see what you want to change, rather than just reacting to a bad week.
  • Motivation is high. A fresh start is a useful push, as long as it turns into a plan rather than a burst of applications that fades by February.

The flip side is that lots of other people have the same idea. More people applying for the same roles means a vague or generic application is even easier to pass over.

What usually decides how it goes

The month you start matters less than a few things that come up again and again.

Knowing what you're moving towards

A search driven only by wanting to leave tends to drift. People apply for anything that looks better than now, and their applications read that way. A clear idea of the kind of role, level and setting you want makes it easier to choose where to apply and to explain why you fit.

A next step that makes sense to an employer

A move up in the same field, a sideways move into a related area and a full change of career are very different searches. An employer reading your application will want the move to make sense. The further the jump, the more you need to show which of your skills and experience carry across.

Fewer, better applications

Sending more applications rarely fixes a search that isn't working. A smaller number of applications aimed at roles that genuinely fit, with your experience described in terms that match each one, usually does better than a high volume of near-identical ones.

Your timing, not just the calendar

Bonus dates, the end of a project, a probation period or a planned holiday can all affect when it makes sense for you to move. These vary from job to job, so they are worth checking before you set a date in your head.

A new year is a good prompt to look. It's a poor reason, on its own, to leave.

The practical basics before you resign

The most common January mistakes aren't about the search at all. They're about leaving. A few points are worth knowing.

  • Notice. GOV.UK says you must give at least a week's notice if you've been in your job for more than a month. Your contract sets your employer's policy on notice, and may say whether it must be in writing. Even when it doesn't, GOV.UK suggests written notice if you think you'll need to refer to it later.
  • Leaving early. GOV.UK warns that you may be in breach of contract if you don't give enough notice, and that your employer could take you to court.
  • Holiday. GOV.UK says employers must pay for untaken statutory leave when you leave, and that you may be able to take what is left during your notice period.
  • Garden leave. Your employer may ask you not to come in, or to work elsewhere, during your notice. GOV.UK says you still get the same pay and contractual benefits.
Don't resign on a conditional offer without thinking it through Many job offers depend on references or other checks. Acas explains that an employer can withdraw a conditional offer if those conditions are not met. Knowing exactly what an offer depends on helps you decide when it's safe to hand in your notice.

References and job offers

Two things often catch people out at the end of a search.

The first is references. Acas says an employer does not have to give a reference by law, except in a few cases: where it has agreed in writing to provide one, for certain regulated financial services jobs, or where it was agreed in another way, such as in a settlement agreement. Where an employer does give references, its policy may limit what goes in them and who can write them. So it helps to think early about who could speak for your work.

The second is the offer itself. Acas explains that a verbal offer is legally the same as a written one, but harder to prove, so getting the details in writing is sensible. A conditional offer depends on things like references or checks. Once you accept and the conditions are met, the contract can have started, and from then on withdrawing could be a breach of contract on either side.

Before your first day Acas says an employer must give you a written statement of your main employment terms on or before your first day of work. It's worth checking it against what you were offered.

Getting it right for your situation

The general picture is the easy part. The hard part is applying it to your own working life: whether you should be looking for the same kind of role or something different, which of your strengths an employer will actually value, what is most likely to hold your applications back, and how to describe your experience so it fits where you want to go. Two people leaving the same job can need very different searches.

Related questions

How much notice do I have to give when I resign?
GOV.UK says you must give at least a week's notice if you've been in your job for more than a month. Your contract sets your employer's policy on notice, so check it before you resign.
Do I get paid for holiday I haven't taken when I leave?
Yes for statutory leave. GOV.UK says the only time you can be paid instead of taking statutory leave is when you leave your job. You may also be able to take what is left during your notice period.
Does my employer have to give me a reference?
Not usually. Acas says an employer does not have to give a reference by law unless it has agreed in writing to provide one, the job is a certain regulated financial services role, or it was agreed in another way, such as in a settlement agreement.
Can a new employer withdraw a job offer after I've accepted it?
With a conditional offer, Acas says the employer can withdraw it if the conditions, such as references or checks, are not met. If all the conditions are met, or the offer was unconditional, withdrawing it could be a breach of contract.
What is garden leave?
GOV.UK describes garden leave as your employer asking you not to come into work, or to work from home or somewhere else, during your notice period. You get the same pay and contractual benefits.
