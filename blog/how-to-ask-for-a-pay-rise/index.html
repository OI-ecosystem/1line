How do I ask for a pay rise, and is it the right time?

Most people who feel underpaid never ask. When they do, it's often after weeks of rehearsing in their head. The good news is that a pay conversation is less about nerve and more about preparation.

25 November 20266 min read
The short answer

In most UK jobs there is no automatic legal right to a pay rise, so a rise usually comes down to a conversation and the case you bring to it. The asks that land tend to be about the value of the work you do now, backed by evidence, raised at a sensible moment and kept calm. A first "no" is often a "not yet", and it's worth knowing the few situations where you do have a right to more pay.

Why "just asking" so often goes nowhere

It's a question we see often on UK work and money forums: someone has been doing more for months, or feels paid less than the job is worth, and wants to know whether to ask, how, and when. The worry is usually the same. Will it sound greedy, and what if the answer is no?

The asks that fail tend to fail for predictable reasons:

  • They're about need, not value. Rising bills or a growing family are real pressures, but they're reasons you want more money, not reasons the business should pay more. Managers can usually only approve a rise they can justify to someone else.
  • They arrive as a surprise. A request made in passing, or in the middle of a busy week, puts your manager on the spot. The easiest answer then is "not now".
  • There's no number. Asking for "a bit more" leaves the other side to decide what that means.
  • They turn into a complaint. Comparing yourself with a named colleague, or hinting at leaving when you don't mean it, can change the tone of the whole conversation.

What usually decides the answer

Every workplace is different, but a few things come up again and again.

The value of what you do now

The strongest cases are about the job as it is today, not the job you were hired for. New responsibilities, a wider remit, results you can point to, or skills the business now relies on all count. Taking on extra duties is one of the clearest reasons to raise pay, because it shows the role itself has changed.

What the role is worth elsewhere

Job adverts, salary surveys and recruiters can give you a sense of the market rate for similar roles in your area. This helps you pick a figure that is realistic rather than hopeful.

How pay is set where you work

Some employers have an annual pay review, fixed pay bands or a union agreement. Others decide case by case. Knowing which applies tells you who actually makes the decision and when budgets are set.

Timing

In general, an ask lands better after a success than after a setback, and before budgets are fixed rather than after. A date already set aside to talk about pay or performance, such as an appraisal, is often easier than a cold request. None of this is a rule, and the right moment depends on your employer.

A pay rise is easier to say yes to when it reads as recognising a change that has already happened.

The few times you do have a right to more pay

Acas explains that an employer doesn't have to give a pay rise unless one of a small number of things applies:

  • The legal minimum wage goes up. If you are paid the National Minimum Wage or National Living Wage, you are entitled to the new rate when the government increases it, which usually happens in April. You are also entitled to a higher rate when you move into an older age band.
  • Your contract gives you one. Some contracts promise a rise when certain conditions are met, such as hitting set objectives. That should appear in your written statement of employment particulars.
  • Custom and practice. Regular rises given over time without a written agreement can sometimes create an entitlement. Acas suggests legal advice if this is unclear.
  • A collective agreement. Where an employer recognises a trade union, pay is usually negotiated with it, and the agreement can cover staff who aren't members.

Equal pay law also applies whenever pay rises are given. Under the Equality Act 2010, men and women must get equal pay for doing equal work.

Talking about pay with colleagues Section 77 of the Equality Act 2010 means a "pay secrecy" term in your contract can't be enforced against you when you discuss pay to find out whether it is linked to a protected characteristic, such as sex or race.

If the answer is no

A refusal isn't always final. It often tells you something useful: that the budget is set for this year, that your manager needs more evidence, or that the decision sits with someone else. It's reasonable to ask what would need to change for the answer to be yes, and when it could be looked at again.

Get it in writing If a rise is agreed, make sure it's confirmed. Acas says an employer must put a change to your main terms in writing within one month of the change. If an agreed rise isn't paid, Acas suggests raising it informally first, then through a formal grievance.

It also helps to know that, according to Acas, asking for a pay rise is unlikely to be a fair reason to dismiss someone. And if you are on maternity leave or long-term sick leave when rises are given, being off work doesn't normally mean you should be left out.

Getting it right for your situation

The general picture is the easy part. The hard part is applying it to your own job: which of your achievements actually make the case, whether now is a good moment at your employer or a poor one, how to put the request so it sounds confident rather than demanding, and what your manager is likely to say back. Two people with the same job title can need very different approaches.

Related questions

Can I be sacked for asking for a pay rise?
Acas says asking for a pay rise is unlikely to be a fair reason to dismiss someone. Employees with two years' service could claim unfair dismissal, and dismissing someone for asking for the legal minimum wage is automatically unfair, with no length-of-service requirement.
I've taken on extra duties. Does that mean I should be paid more?
Not automatically, unless your contract says so. But a bigger role is one of the strongest reasons to ask, because it shows the job has changed. It also helps to be clear whether the new duties are temporary or permanent.
Can I talk to colleagues about what they earn?
Under section 77 of the Equality Act 2010, a contract term that stops you discussing your pay is unenforceable when the discussion is to find out whether pay is linked to a protected characteristic, such as sex or race. You are also protected from being victimised for it.
Will I miss out on a pay rise if I'm on maternity or sick leave?
Usually not. Acas says excluding someone on statutory maternity pay from a pay rise could be maternity discrimination, and that a rise given to all staff should also go to someone on long-term sick leave.
What if my manager agrees but nothing changes?
Changes to your main terms, including pay, should be confirmed in writing. By law an employer must do this within one month of the change. If an agreed rise isn't paid, Acas suggests raising it informally first, then through a grievance.
