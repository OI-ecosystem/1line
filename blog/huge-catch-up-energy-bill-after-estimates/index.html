Bills & money

I’ve had a huge catch-up energy bill after estimated readings. Do I have to pay it?

Months of steady payments, then a bill for hundreds or more. Catch-up bills are common after estimated readings, but there is a limit on how far back a supplier can go, and you may need to raise it yourself.

15 November 20266 min read
The short answer

Usually only part of it. Under Ofgem’s back-billing rules, you don’t normally have to pay for gas or electricity used more than 12 months ago if you were never accurately billed for it, never told about it in a statement, or your direct debit was set too low to cover it. Energy used within the last year is still owed, and the protection can be lost if you acted unreasonably, for example by blocking access to your meter.

Why a catch-up bill lands out of nowhere

It’s a question we see often on UK social media and money forums. Someone has paid a steady amount for months, then a bill arrives asking for hundreds, sometimes more than a thousand pounds. Others notice the opposite first: no gas bill for a long time, and a nagging sense that one is coming.

The usual cause is the gap between estimated and actual readings. The Energy Ombudsman explains that when a supplier can’t get an actual reading, it has to rely on estimates. If those estimates are lower than what you really used, the shortfall quietly builds up. When an actual reading finally arrives, the supplier recalculates and sends the difference as one bill.

The Ombudsman lists some of the reasons this happens:

  • Meters going unread for long periods.
  • Faulty first-generation smart meters being replaced, or meters not sending the right data.
  • Readings rejected by the supplier’s systems, so estimates are used even though you gave actual readings.
  • Empty or hard-to-reach properties where no one can read the meter.

When Ofgem brought in the rules in 2018, it said catch-up bills came either from billing system problems or from suppliers estimating bills until an actual reading showed higher use.

The 12-month back-billing rule

Ofgem banned domestic suppliers from issuing back-bills for energy used more than 12 months ago. The rule came into effect for households in May 2018. Ofgem’s guidance says you don’t have to pay for energy used more than 12 months ago if:

  • you haven’t had an accurate bill for it before,
  • you haven’t been told about the amount in a statement of account before, or
  • your direct debit was previously set too low to cover it.

The Energy Ombudsman adds that the limit covers standing charges as well as energy used, and that the 12 months run back from the date on the new bill.

Only the older part drops away The rule doesn’t cancel a catch-up bill. It limits how far back it can reach. Energy you used in the last 12 months is still owed, even if the bill for it is late.

When the protection doesn’t apply

The rules have limits, and they are where many disagreements start.

  • Acting unreasonably. Ofgem says you will have to pay for older energy if you acted unreasonably, for example by stopping your supplier from billing you accurately, including by blocking access to your meter, ignoring requests for payment, or stealing gas or electricity.
  • Bills you were sent but didn’t pay. Citizens Advice says the protection doesn’t apply if the supplier sent you a bill before the year passed and you didn’t pay it.

Not sending meter readings isn’t the same as obstruction. When it announced the rules, Ofgem said consumers would not be at fault for failing to provide meter readings, and that suppliers would need to look at behaviour case by case. The exception was aimed at obstruction, such as blocking physical access to a meter, or clearly unreasonable conduct, such as tampering with a meter.

Don’t assume it has been applied None of the official guidance says the limit will be applied automatically. Ofgem, Citizens Advice and the Energy Ombudsman all describe the customer contacting the supplier if they think the rules haven’t been followed.

Making sense of the bill itself

Catch-up bills are often hard to follow, because they rework months of charges at once. Before deciding whether it’s right, the Energy Ombudsman suggests looking at:

  1. The dates the bill covers. Check the earliest date, and any split sections or tariff changes along the way.
  2. The total and any deductions. Look at what’s being asked for and whether payments you’ve already made are taken off.
  3. The readings behind it. You can ask the supplier for a breakdown showing the meter readings and how the charges were worked out.

The most common question is simply whether the usage is real. A bill built on a reading that is wrong, or on the wrong meter, is a different problem from a correct bill that arrived late. Comparing the supplier’s readings with what your meter shows today is often the quickest way to tell which you have.

If you disagree, or can’t pay

If you think the back-billing rules haven’t been followed, Ofgem says to contact your supplier right away, and that money taken in error will be refunded. Citizens Advice suggests writing to the supplier to say you’re protected by the rules and should only be charged for one year’s use, and making a formal complaint if they still ask for the full amount.

The Energy Ombudsman says that if the issue isn’t resolved within 8 weeks, or you get a “deadlock letter”, you can bring the dispute to it for a free, impartial review. Our article on what to do when your energy bill is wrong covers that route in more detail.

Even a correct bill can be hard to find the money for. Citizens Advice says you can ask your supplier for a payment plan, paying fixed amounts over a set period to cover what you owe plus what you use now. The supplier must take into account your income, outgoings, debts and circumstances when agreeing it. If you pay by direct debit, it may raise your payments to recover the amount over a few months instead.

Getting it right for your situation

The rule is simple to state and harder to apply. Which part of your bill falls outside the 12 months? Was there an earlier bill or statement that changes things? Is the usage real, or built on a bad reading? And if part of it is owed, how does that fit with everything else your household is paying for this winter?

Usually not. The exception is where you acted unreasonably, for example by blocking access to your meter.
Do I still have to pay for the last 12 months?
Yes. The back-billing rule limits how far back a catch-up bill can go. Energy you used within the last 12 months is still owed, even if the bill for it is late.
Does not sending meter readings mean I lose the protection?
Not on its own. When it introduced the rules, Ofgem said consumers would not be at fault for failing to provide meter readings. The exception is aimed at obstruction, such as blocking access to the meter, or clearly unreasonable behaviour like tampering or stealing energy.
